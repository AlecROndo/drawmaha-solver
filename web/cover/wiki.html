Overview · Getting started

Introduction

What the Drawmaha solver is, the rules of the game it is built for, and the terms used throughout this wiki.

The Drawmaha solver is a game-theory solver for heads-up pot-limit Drawmaha, a split-pot hybrid of draw poker and Omaha that no existing solver plays. It does not attack the full game head-on. It is built as a validation ladder: a sequence of games, each one adding a single new idea to the last, and each one graded against a known answer before the next is attempted. By the time the method meets the real game, every piece of it has been checked somewhere a mistake would have shown.

The question every step answers is the same: how much would a perfect adversary win against this strategy? That number, exploitability, is zero exactly at a Nash equilibrium and positive everywhere else, and it is computed exactly by a grader that shares no code with the solver. Training loss is never used as a progress meter, because in self-play it can fall while the strategy gets no better.

The game

If you have never played Drawmaha, this is the minimum you need to follow the rest of the wiki. The target game, as the README's "The target game" section defines it:

  • Each player is dealt 5 hole cards.
  • It is a bomb pot: both players ante and the flop comes out with no preflop betting. The order is ante → flop (3 cards) → betting → one draw (discard 0–5 cards and replace them) → turn → betting → river → betting. Three betting rounds, not Omaha's four.
  • The draw-1 rule: a player who draws exactly one card is dealt it face up; they may keep it or reject it and take the next card face down.
  • Split pot: half the pot goes to the best Omaha hand (exactly 2 hole cards + 3 board cards), half to the best 5-card poker hand made of the hole cards alone.

The solve's defaults, all configurable: pot-limit with the action set {fold, check/call, pot}; 100 big blinds effective; both players ante, so there are no blinds and no positional asymmetry from them. Bet sizes are a config, so a multi-size solve is a re-solve, not a rewrite.

Why it is hard: a hand is judged two ways at once, the draw changes both of them, and the draw-1 rule leaks information publicly. A strategy has to balance what it holds for the Omaha half against what it holds for the draw half, and the size of the full game rules out the table-based methods that solve the small games below. See Abstraction for how the project shrinks it, and Full Drawmaha for what is still to be done. The live rules page is at /rules.

Game-theory glossary

TermMeaning
Strategy (σ)For every situation a player can be in, a probability for each legal action. A strategy that mixes is a mixed strategy; one that always picks one action per situation is pure.
Information set (infoset)Everything the acting player knows at a decision: their own cards and the public history, never the opponent's cards. A strategy is defined per infoset, because a player cannot act differently in two situations they cannot tell apart.
Chance nodeA point where the deck acts rather than a player: a deal, a board card, a draw.
Nash equilibriumA pair of strategies where neither player gains by changing their own. In a two-player zero-sum game, playing one guarantees at least the game's value against anyone.
Game valueWhat a seat wins per hand when both play an equilibrium. Kuhn poker's first seat is worth −1/18.
Best response (BR)The strategy that wins the most against one fixed opponent strategy.
ExploitabilityThe average of the two seats' best-response winnings against a strategy pair, (BR₀ + BR₁) / 2. Zero at equilibrium. The project's only grade.
RegretHow much better an action would have done than what the strategy actually did, accumulated over iterations.
Regret matchingPlay each action in proportion to its positive accumulated regret.
CFRCounterfactual regret minimisation: regret matching at every infoset of a game tree at once, with each update weighted by how likely the others make it to reach that infoset.
Average strategyThe reach-weighted average of every iteration's strategy. It is CFR's answer: the average converges to equilibrium; the current strategy need not.
MCCFRMonte Carlo CFR: CFR on sampled hands instead of the whole tree, for games too large to enumerate.
Deep CFRCFR with the per-infoset regret tables replaced by a neural network that generalises across infosets.

Key concepts

These are the ideas the solver is built from. Each links to its full treatment in the Theory tab.

ConceptWhat it is
Nash equilibrium and exploitabilityThe target, and the meter. Exploitability measures distance from equilibrium in the units that matter, chips an adversary takes. See Equilibrium and Exploitability.

AbstractionMini-drawmaha keeps Drawmaha's structure (a bomb pot, a draw, a pot that pays twice) on a deck small enough to grade exactly. See Abstraction.

Method

The validation ladder, the pre-registered evaluation protocol, and what each rung found.

The full game is too large to solve on the first try, and a solver that is wrong does not announce it: a swapped weight or a peeking grader still produces a strategy, a falling curve and a confident number. The project's answer is to never take a step that cannot be checked. Each rung is a game that adds exactly one new idea to the rung below it, and each is graded against an answer that was known before the solver ran.

Each rung adds one idea and is graded before the next

Exploitability is the only grade

The evaluation protocol was fixed in the README before the results existed ("Evaluation protocol (pre-registered)"). The reasoning: in self-play, training loss is not a progress meter. Both players move together, so a loss can fall while the pair stays easy to beat. The only honest question is what an adversary wins.

exploitability(σ) = (BR₀(σ₁) + BR₁(σ₀)) / 2
  • BR₀(σ₁) is what seat 0 wins per hand by playing its best response against seat 1's strategy, and BR₁(σ₀) the same for seat 1.
  • Why the average of two: the game is zero-sum, so at an equilibrium the two best responses are the game value and its negative and the sum is exactly zero. Any leak in either seat's strategy pushes one of them up, so the average is positive everywhere else.
  • What moves it: a strategy that leaks more chips to a perfect opponent in either seat raises it; it cannot be lowered by playing well against a weak opponent. That is what makes it a meter and not a score.

How it is computed at each rung:

  • Toys (rungs 0–2): exactly, and checked against Kuhn's closed form and an OpenSpiel solve of Leduc.
  • Mini-drawmaha (rungs 3–4): the exact best-response walk in src/drawmaha_solver/minidrawmaha/exploitability.py as ground truth, plus the Deep CFR versus tabular gap.
  • Full scale: a duplicate-dealt head-to-head ladder of checkpoints plus a trained reinforcement-learning exploiter against the frozen network, reported as a lower bound on exploitability, because no exact walk is possible there.

Units are total chips across both pot halves, per hand, with per-half EV and scoop rate kept as split-pot diagnostics. The grader never imports the solver — see Onboarding.

Warning

The current strategy is not the answer. At rungs 1 and 2 its exploitability sits near 0.2 and between 0.75 and 1.48 chips/hand for the whole run while the average strategy's falls steadily (the README's Kuhn and Leduc tables). Every surface that plays a strategy reads an average, never the final iterate.

Architecture

How the solver packages, the graders, the analysis commands, the committed figures and the site fit together.

The project is one Python package that solves games, one directory of committed outputs, and one website that draws those outputs. Data flows in one direction: Python computes, writes a file into the repository, and every downstream surface reads that file. Nothing on the site re-implements the solver, and nothing in a grader imports it.

Data flows one way: solve, grade, export, draw

  1. Rules — each game's game.py (for mini-drawmaha, the rules/ subpackage) defines the state machine, the infosets and the payoffs, with no solver in it.
  2. Solver — CFR (cfr.py, rungs 1–2), sampled lockstep MCCFR (minidrawmaha/solver/, rung 3) or Deep CFR (deepcfr/, rung 4) trains the regret ledgers and accumulates the average strategy.
  3. Grader — each rung's exploitability.py imports the rules alone and computes the exact best response against the solver's average.
  4. Analysis and export — entry points registered in pyproject.toml (kuhn-analysis, minidraw-analysis, deep-analysis, minidraw-deals, minidraw-ranges, …) write figures to figures/<rung>/ and JSON exports to the apps' public/ folders.
  5. Site — five Vite + React apps and five static pages read those files. The static pages are written by scripts/site/make_static_pages.py from the same committed numbers; Vercel builds and serves everything.

Heavy runs (the rung 3 race and the rung 4 arms) train on Modal or on a Mac, write checkpoints to Modal volumes or a local directory, and come back into the repository only as graded JSON under figures/ and as release assets on GitHub.

Note

The layering inside a rung is always the same: the rules → the ledgers (infoset_table.py) → the solver → the meter (exploitability.py) → the entry points (analysis.py, play.py, exploit_server.py). Reading any rung in that order works (src/drawmaha_solver/AGENTS.md).

Quickstart

Install the package, run the suite, solve a game, play against it, and see the site locally.

Everything below runs from the repository root with uv. Python 3.12 or later; NumPy and matplotlib are the only heavy dependencies of the default install, and torch comes in only with the deep extra.

Install and run the suite

uv sync                  # Python 3.12+; add --extra deep for torch (rung 4 training only)
uv run pytest            # the whole suite; needs no torch, Modal, OpenSpiel or network

The full default suite takes about seven minutes on a laptop (tests/AGENTS.md). The fast loop is one rung's directory with -x:

uv run pytest -x tests/kuhn

Solve a game and redraw its figures

Each rung has an analysis command that solves it and rewrites its figures under figures/<rung>/. Rungs 0 to 2 run unseeded and deterministically, so a clean run leaves the working tree clean; a changed number shows up in git diff.

CommandWhat it doesTime
uv run rps-analysisrock-paper-scissors self-play and the biased-opponent run → figures/rung0/seconds
uv run kuhn-analysisKuhn to 100k iterations, 60 checkpoints → figures/rung1/, prints the answer sheetseconds
uv run leduc-analysisLeduc to 2,000 iterations → figures/rung2/about two minutes
uv run minidraw-analysisreads the committed grades and the frozen release → figures/rung3/ and answer_sheet.jsonabout two minutes
uv run minidraw-exploit-by-reach --release tabularwhere mini-drawmaha's exploitability lives, binned by reachminutes; downloads the release
uv run deep-analysisthe rung 4 price figure → figures/rung4/price.png and answer_sheet.jsonquick; reads committed grades

minidraw-analysis downloads the frozen strategy (rung3-lcfr-2m, 18 MB compressed) once and checks it against a SHA-256 pinned in src/drawmaha_solver/minidrawmaha/answer/strategy.py.

Play against the solver in the terminal

uv run rps-play          # against the regret-matching learner
uv run kuhn-play         # against Kuhn's equilibrium; seats alternate each hand
uv run leduc-play        # against Leduc's solved strategy
uv run minidraw-play     # against the frozen mini-drawmaha strategy

Every bot plays the average strategy, reads only its own infoset, and reveals both hands at the end, folds included.

Lock a strategy and watch CFR exploit it

The Kuhn and Leduc pages have an Exploit tab that posts your locked strategy to a local Python server and draws the counter-strategy CFR finds.

uv run kuhn-exploit-server            # the page POSTs locked spots to this
cd web/rung1-viz && npm install && npm run dev   # then http://localhost:5173/rung1/#exploit

leduc-exploit-server with web/rung2-viz works the same way.

See the site

An app alone:

cd web/rung3-viz && npm install && npm run dev

The whole site, wired the way Vercel wires it:

bash scripts/site/vercel_build.sh          # builds public/: five npm ci builds, then the static pages
uv run python scripts/site/serve_site.py   # http://localhost:4321

After changing anything a static page reads — including this wiki — regenerate the pages:

uv run python scripts/site/make_static_pages.py

Warning

Do not run the Modal launchers to try things out. scripts/minidrawmaha/modal_rung3.py and scripts/deepcfr/modal_rung4.py start paid containers, and they are launched by a person. See Modal.

Onboarding

The rules every change in the repository follows, why each exists, and how to add a new surface.

The rules below come from the repository's AGENTS.md files: the root one, which holds what applies to every change, and nested ones loaded when you work inside a subtree (src/drawmaha_solver/, src/drawmaha_solver/minidrawmaha/, src/drawmaha_solver/deepcfr/, tests/, scripts/, web/, docs/). Each rule exists because breaking it fails silently: the code still runs and still produces a confident number.

The rules that hold everywhere

A grader never shares code with what it grades

Every rung's exploitability.py imports the game rules and nothing from the solver. A bug shared between the two would appear in both and cancel, and the meter would certify the thing that broke it. A review of a grader starts with its import block.

Inside a grader, the order of operations is fixed: sum over the hidden worlds first, then take the responder's max. A max inside the sum is an adversary who sees face-down cards. At Kuhn, a test pins that a peeking meter scores a genuine Nash equilibrium at 0.278 chips/hand. At mini-drawmaha, grouping the responder's max by the table's key instead of by physical state under-reports by about 0.012 chips/hand (src/drawmaha_solver/minidrawmaha/AGENTS.md). See Exploitability.

CFR's two reach weights are keyword-only

RegretMatcher.update(utilities, *, regret_weight, strategy_weight) in src/drawmaha_solver/regret_matching.py. Regret is weighted by the counterfactual reach π₋ᵢ (chance and the opponent), the average by the player's own reach πᵢ. Swapped, every rung still converges — to a wrong answer, with no warning. At Kuhn the swapped run folds the king to a bet 18% of the time. Never make them positional. See CFR.

Committed fixtures, figures and exports are regenerated, never hand-edited

tests/leduc/referee.json, tests/minidrawmaha/census.json, everything under figures/, the packs under web/rung3-viz/public/deals/ and web/solver-viz/public/mini/, and the copies under web/*/src/data/. Each names the command that writes it. The tests re-derive the numbers in them a second way, so a fixture that merely asserted itself would pass while the solver rotted. The static pages, this wiki included, are regenerated by scripts/site/make_static_pages.py.

CI runs no tests

The only GitHub workflow is an automated review. uv run pytest is the gate, and you run it yourself before every PR, plus npm test in any app you touched. The default suite must pass without torch, Modal, OpenSpiel or a network download.

Docstrings and the README are the design record

The reviewer re-derives the math a docstring states. A wrong claim in a docstring or in README.md is a bug, fixed in the same change as the code. Convergence numbers quoted in either are recomputed by a test from committed data, never transcribed from a session.

PRs branch from their predecessor's head and target main

Never stack a PR on another's branch, and never rebase a branch onto main to clean its diff: that deletes the modules it imports. Commit subjects state the finding:

  • feat(rung4): <the finding>
  • fix(web): <the finding>

Modal costs money

The launchers under scripts/ that run on Modal are started by a person, never by an agent on its own initiative. Deploy first (free), then run. See Modal.

Never git stash

Several agents work in parallel worktrees that share one stash stack; a bare git stash pop can take another session's changes.

Nash equilibrium in two-player zero-sum games

What the solver is trying to find, why it is the right target for heads-up poker, and how the repo measures distance from it.

Every game in this repo is two-player and zero-sum: whatever one seat wins, the other loses. In that setting there is one well-defined thing a solver can aim for — a Nash equilibrium, a pair of strategies where neither seat can gain by changing its own play while the other holds still. This page says what that object is, why it is the right target for heads-up Drawmaha, and what it does not promise, using the solved games the repo grades against.

A strategy is a probability for every action at every spot

Poker is a game of imperfect information: you see your own cards and the betting, not your opponent's cards. So a strategy cannot depend on the full state of the deal. It depends on an information set (an infoset, or spot): everything the acting player can see at a decision.

  • In Kuhn poker an infoset is the actor's own card plus the public betting history — K with history pb (check, bet) is one spot. Two deals that differ only in the opponent's card are the same infoset, and must map to one strategy entry.
  • A behavioural strategy σᵢ for seat i assigns, at each of its infosets I, a probability σᵢ(I, a) to every legal action a. Probabilities at one infoset sum to 1.
  • A strategy profile σ = (σ₀, σ₁) is one strategy per seat.

The expected payoff of a profile is uᵢ(σ): average over the deck's deals and both seats' action probabilities, in chips per hand. Zero-sum means u₀(σ) = −u₁(σ) for every σ.

Equilibrium means no unilateral improvement

u₀(σ₀*, σ₁*) ≥ u₀(σ₀, σ₁*) for every σ₀
u₁(σ₀*, σ₁*) ≥ u₁(σ₀*, σ₁) for every σ₁
  • σ₀\, σ₁\ are the equilibrium strategies.
  • The first line says: holding P1 at σ₁\, nothing P0 can switch to does better than σ₀\. The second says the same for P1.
  • Neither line mentions what the opponent should do — each is about one seat deviating alone.

Why this is the right target for heads-up poker

In a two-player zero-sum game the minimax theorem makes the equilibrium payoff a single number, the game value v. Every equilibrium earns exactly v for P0, and playing any equilibrium strategy guarantees at least v whatever the opponent does. That guarantee is the reason to want one: an equilibrium strategy cannot lose more than the game's built-in edge, against anyone.

The negative values all have the same cause: the second seat acts last. In mini-drawmaha P1 acts last in both betting rounds and in the draw, and that is worth about a tenth of an ante.

The equilibrium can be a family, not a point

Kuhn poker shows that "the" equilibrium is often a set. The first player's equilibria form a one-parameter family over α ∈ [0, ⅓]:

  • with the jack, P0 bluffs (opens with a bet) with probability α;
  • with the king, P0 value-bets with probability 3α;
  • with the queen facing a check–bet, P0 calls with probability α + ⅓.

Every α in the range earns exactly −1/18. The solver lands wherever its iterates take it — α = 0.221 and K-open = 0.661 ≈ 3α in the README's run — and the ratio is discovered, never supplied. That is why tests/kuhn pins the nine infosets that are the same in every equilibrium and the two invariants (K-open = 3α, Qpb = α + ⅓), and never a hard-coded α.

Note

A family of equilibria is why a strategy is never graded by comparing its numbers to "the answer". It is graded by exploitability — what a perfect adversary wins against it — which is zero for every member of the family and positive for everything else. See Exploitability.

Nash is unbeatable, not punishing

An equilibrium guarantees v; it does not collect what a weak opponent leaves on the table. The Kuhn exploit tab measures the gap. Take a P1 that plays equilibrium everywhere except two leaks — it calls a bet with the queen 90% of the time instead of a third, and folds the king 40% of the time:

Theory · Equilibrium and regret

Regret matching

The learning rule at the heart of every solver in the repo — one ledger per spot, unchanged from rock-paper-scissors to mini-drawmaha.

Regret matching (Hart & Mas-Colell 2000) is how a player who repeats a game learns to play it without being told the answer. The repo uses one implementation of it, RegretMatcher in regret_matching.py, for every game. Rock-paper-scissors feeds it a payoff vector each round. Kuhn, Leduc and mini-drawmaha feed it one vector per infoset per visit. The rule itself is the same in all of them; only what feeds it grows.

The whole algorithm is three lines

  1. Record regret. After a visit, for every action a, add how much better always-a would have done than the mix you actually played.
  2. Play in proportion to positive regret. Next visit, play each action with probability proportional to its accumulated positive regret; uniform when none is positive.
  3. Answer with the average. The running average of the strategies played converges to equilibrium in two-player zero-sum games. The current strategy does not.
r(a) = u(a) − ⟨σ, u⟩
R(a) ← R(a) + r(a)
σ(a) = R⁺(a) / Σₐ′ R⁺(a′), R⁺ = max(R, 0)
  • u(a) is the payoff action a would have earned this visit. In RPS it is a column of the payoff matrix against what the opponent threw; in a tree it is a counterfactual value computed by the walk (see CFR).
  • ⟨σ, u⟩ = Σₐ σ(a)·u(a) is what the current mix earned on the same visit — the baseline every action is compared against.
  • r(a) is the regret of not having played a: positive if a beat the mix, negative if it did worse.
  • R(a) is cumulative_regret, the ledger's memory. σ is derived from it on demand and never stored.

Why positive regret, and why proportional

The rule puts probability where playing more would have helped. An action with negative cumulative regret has, on balance, done worse than the mix, and gets probability zero until it climbs back above zero. Among the positive ones, an action with twice the regret gets twice the probability. When nothing is positive there is no evidence to follow, so the ledger falls back to uniform.

What moves what. Feed in one large positive r for an action, and that action's probability jumps next visit. Feed in a long run of small negatives and its probability drops to zero, and stays there until enough positive regret accumulates to cancel the debt. Plain regret matching keeps that debt in full. CFR+, compared in Regret rules, floors it at zero instead.

The average converges; the current strategy cycles

The current σ chases whatever beat it last time, so against an adapting opponent it cycles: rock gets punished by paper, paper by scissors, and round it goes. The average of those strategies converges.

The ledger therefore keeps a second vector, strategy_sum, which banks σ after every visit. average_strategy() normalises it. In rock-paper-scissors self-play the average reaches (0.334, 0.333, 0.333) after 100,000 rounds, exploitable for 0.0009 chips/round, and its exploitability falls like 1/√T. Against a fixed opponent that throws rock half the time, the same ledger converges to pure paper and earns +0.24/round; the best response earns +0.25.

Warning

Every solver in the repo answers with the average, and every grader grades the average. Kuhn's table in CFR shows the current iterate between 0.20 and 0.28 chips/hand exploitable however long the run goes, and Leduc's committed solve ends with the current iterate at 0.97 against the average's 0.011 (web/rung2-viz/src/data/solve.json). This is also why rung 4 has to rebuild an average explicitly — see Deep CFR.

Two weights let the same ledger serve a game tree

In RPS every round counts the same. In a tree a spot is only reached some of the time, and the two vectors want different scales:

WeightMultipliesIsWhy
regret_weightthe regret r added to Rthe counterfactual reach π₋ᵢ: chance's and the opponent's probabilities of reaching the spot, leaving out your owna line you currently avoid must keep learning at full strength, or you could never discover it is good

Warning

Swapped, both versions run silently. On Kuhn, swapping the two weights still completes, still prints a converged-looking grid, and folds the king to a bet 18% of the time, with game value −0.00001 instead of −1/18. That is why update(utilities, *, regret_weight, strategy_weight) takes them keyword-only, and why a test pins that the two scales are distinguishable. The repo's root instructions forbid making them positional.

The ledger contract

From mini-drawmaha on, many things touch a ledger: the walk, the regret rules, the checkpoint, the packed table that holds 6.2M of them in flat arrays. The module docstring fixes exactly which slots they may use:

Counterfactual regret minimisation

How CFR turns a game tree into one regret-matching ledger per infoset, and why the two reach weights must never be swapped.

Counterfactual regret minimisation (CFR) runs regret matching at every infoset of a game tree at once, and its average strategy converges to a Nash equilibrium. RPS needs nothing more than the ledger: the opponent shows a move, and the payoff matrix already has a column of utilities for it. A tree doesn't give you that. What "bet with the king" is worth depends on everything both players do afterwards, so the utilities have to be computed, by walking the tree. That walk is the one new idea in Kuhn, in kuhn/cfr.py, and Leduc and mini-drawmaha keep its shape.

Two quantities travel through the walk in opposite directions

One CFR iteration is a recursive walk from the root. At every node it carries reach probabilities down and returns values up.

  • Down: reach. reach[p] is the product of player p's own action probabilities on the path so far, and nothing else. Chance is in neither entry: its probability travels separately and is multiplied into the counterfactual reach π₋ᵢ (chance × opponent) when a ledger is banked, as below.
  • Up: values. What the node is worth to each seat, as a plain conditional expectation: at a decision node, Σₐ σ(I, a) · v(child a). Values are not weighted by reach. Reach weights are applied once, when a ledger is banked.
  • The walk returns the value to both seats, so nothing is negated on the way up.

At an infoset I owned by player i, the walk has a value vᵢ(I, a) for each action and the mix's value vᵢ(I) = Σₐ σ(I, a)·vᵢ(I, a). It banks:

R(I, a) += π₋ᵢ(h) · ( vᵢ(h, a) − vᵢ(h) )
S(I, a) += πᵢ(h) · σ(I, a)
  • h is the concrete history being walked. Many histories share one infoset, because they differ only in cards player i cannot see. The bank adds across all of them.
  • π₋ᵢ(h), the counterfactual reach, is the probability that chance and the opponent bring play to h. Player i's own choices are left out on purpose: it is the reach i would have if i played to get there.
  • πᵢ(h) is player i's own reach, the probability i's own choices lead to h.
  • R is cumulative_regret, S is strategy_sum. The average strategy is S(I, ·) normalised.

Why the regret uses the opponent's reach and the average uses yours

Regret asks "had I played to get here, how much would always-a have beaten my mix?" Weighting it by your own reach would silence a line you currently avoid: its regret would be multiplied by roughly zero, and you could never find out it is good. The counterfactual weight keeps every line learning at full strength.

The average asks a different question: "what did I actually play?" A spot you almost never steer into should barely affect your average, so it is weighted by your own reach.

Warning

Swap the two weights and every rung still runs. On Kuhn the swapped walk prints a converged-looking grid that folds the king to a bet 18% of the time, with game value −0.00001 instead of −1/18. Nothing crashes. That is why RegretMatcher.update takes regret_weight= and strategy_weight= as keyword-only arguments.

Why chance is in π₋ᵢ, and why it does not matter in Kuhn

The counterfactual reach is defined to include chance's probability. In Kuhn every deal is equally likely (1/6), so the chance factor scales every regret increment by the same constant. Regret matching reads only the ratio of positive regrets, so σ, the reach and the average are unchanged; the repo measures the difference at 9e-15 after 5,000 iterations, which is floating-point rounding. The factor is still carried because once a game deals non-uniform chance it stops being a common scale. It is also why the ledgers hold numbers 6× smaller than Neller & Lanctot's reference trace, whose pseudocode leaves the factor out.

Two lessons, and the repo builds on both:

  • The average falls steadily; the current strategy sits around 0.2 forever. At 1,000 iterations it is briefly worse than at 10. Vanilla CFR's current iterate cycles. Every grader grades the average, and the live surfaces serve averages.
  • Game value is the softer grader. At 100 iterations the game value is within 0.0009 of −1/18 and looks solved, while the strategy is still 15× more exploitable than it will be at 10,000. What changes at Leduc: chance in the middle, ragged action sets

Leduc keeps the walk and adds two things:

  • A chance node mid-hand. The board card comes after the first betting round, so the walk meets chance inside the tree rather than enumerating every deal at the top. A jack is trash until a jack hits the board, then it's the best hand in the game.
  • Spots with different action sets. Facing a raise you may fold, call or raise; at the open you may not fold. A ledger is 2 or 3 wide depending on where it sits, and column 0 means call at the open but fold facing a raise. Every loop pairs enumerate(legal_actions()) with the ledger's columns.

288 infosets instead of Kuhn's 12. One iteration walks 9,450 nodes in about 50 ms. Vanilla CFR enumerates the whole tree and never samples, so two runs agree bit for bit, and figures/rung1/ and figures/rung2/ double as regression fingerprints.

Locking a seat turns CFR into a best-response finder

The same walk can hold some infosets fixed. At a locked infoset it plays the supplied probabilities and banks nothing; elsewhere it learns as usual. The locked probabilities still advance the reach, so they still enter the learner's π₋ᵢ. The learner is never told the opponent is fixed. It is simply weighted by how often that opponent brings it to each spot. Minimising regret against an opponent that can't move back converges to a best response, not to Nash. Lock a whole seat and CFR becomes an exploit finder with no new algorithm. That is the Exploit tab at /rung1#exploit, graded against the exact best response (see Exploitability).

Past Leduc, the full walk does not run

Mini-drawmaha has 85.1 billion decision nodes and 6,220,050 infosets. A full walk per iteration is impossible, so rung 3 samples the walk instead. The regret weight then becomes the sampling frequency, which is the subject of MCCFR.

Exploitability and the exact best response

The one number every result in the repo is graded by — what a perfect adversary wins — and the rules that keep the grader honest.

In self-play, training loss doesn't tell you how close you are. Two players improving against each other can drift to somewhere comfortable for both and beatable by anyone else, and still report a fine game value to each other. The honest question is how much a perfect adversary would win. That number is exploitability. Every result in the README, on /benchmarks and on /research is exploitability, computed exactly and never estimated.

Exploitability is the mean of the two best-response values

exploitability(σ) = ( BR₀(σ₁) + BR₁(σ₀) ) / 2
  • BR₀(σ₁) is the most seat 0 could earn, in chips per hand, against seat 1 frozen at σ₁. BR₁(σ₀) is the same for seat 1 against seat 0's half.
  • Freezing one side and re-optimising the other removes the cancellation that makes self-play numbers flatter themselves.
  • At a Nash equilibrium the two terms are v and −v, the game value and its negative, so the sum is exactly 0. Any slack pushes it above zero.
  • Halving averages over the two seats, so one number grades the whole profile.

What the number does and does not mean

Exploitability is not what the strategy loses to any one opponent. It is the average of the two best responders' edges. For scale, uniform random play on mini-drawmaha is exploitable for 4.842 chips/hand (figures/rung4/answer_sheet.json). The frozen tabular answer is at 0.061346, with BR₀ = −0.034421 and BR₁ = +0.157114. P0's best response is still negative: even a perfect P0 loses against the solved P1, because the second seat's edge is built into the game.

It is also the stricter grader. In Kuhn at 100 iterations the game value is within 0.0009 of −1/18 while the strategy is still 15 times more exploitable than at 10,000 (see CFR).

Sum over hidden worlds first, then take the max

The responder plays from infosets, and must choose one action per infoset without seeing the cards hidden from it. So the best-response value at an infoset I of seat i is:

BRᵢ(I) = maxₐ Σ_{h ∈ I} π_c(h) · π₋ᵢ(h) · v(h · a)
  • h ∈ I ranges over the hidden worlds the infoset stands for: the deals that look identical to the responder.
  • π_c(h) · π₋ᵢ(h) weights each world by how often the deck and the frozen opponent produce it.
  • v(h · a) is what action a leads to in that world, with the responder playing its best responses deeper down.
  • The max is outside the sum. Score each action across all worlds, then pick one. The responder's own reach is the same for every world in one infoset under perfect recall, so it is left out: it would only rescale the argmax's inputs uniformly.

Warning

A max inside the sum is an adversary who sees face-down cards. It picks a different action per world, which no real opponent can do, and it overstates exploitability. The repo pins the size of that bug. A peeking meter scores a genuine Kuhn Nash equilibrium at 0.278 chips/hand instead of 0, and on Leduc the per-(infoset, opponent hand) max read 115× the honest number. A peeking grader sends you hunting for a bug in a correct solver. Root instructions: "Sum over the hidden worlds first, then take the responder's max."

A grader never shares code with what it grades

Every game's exploitability.py imports the game's rules and nothing from the solver. If the walk and the meter shared a routine, a bug in it would appear in both and cancel, and the meter would certify the thing that broke it. Every result's ground truth is independent of the solver:

Why enumerating pure strategies keeps Kuhn's adversary honest

A pure strategy names one action per infoset, fixed before any card is dealt. "Maximise per deal" can't even be written in that form. That is what made the 64-strategy enumeration a safe grader at Kuhn. At Leduc there are too many to enumerate, so the same structure is spelled out as the sum-then-max recursion.

Mini-drawmaha's meter walks the public tree with vectors

85.1 billion decision nodes rule out tagging every world. The meter moves along the public tree instead (141 public decision points), carrying one vector over the opponent's private states and one over the responder's:

v[b, r] = Σₕ w[b, h] · M_b[r, h] at a terminal, board b
v[b, r] = maxₐ vₐ[b, r] at a responder spot
  • r is one of the responder's private states, its physical (hole, thrown card): 455 × 16 = 7,280 entries.
  • h is one of the opponent's private states, indexed the same way.
  • w[b, h], the reach vector, says how often the deck and the frozen opponent bring exactly h here on board b.
  • M_b[r, h] is what r is paid against h on that board (the split-pot showdown), zero where the two hands share a card.
  • b is the physical board, a batch axis: 15 after the first card, 210 ordered pairs after the second.

One entry (b, r) is a responder infoset, and the opponent's hands were already summed inside it by the first line. So one max per entry is sum-first-max-second, and the peeking bug can't be written: no vector ever indexes the responder's state and the opponent's hand together. Vectors are indexed by physical cards, not canonical keys, because the two seats' canonical keys for the same deal usually sit in different suit frames and could not be dotted together.

Where the exploitability lives: a per-spot decomposition

A best responder's edge telescopes over its own decisions as one-step deviations. At each infoset, multiply how often the strategy's own play reaches it by what the best response earns there over the strategy's mix. Every spot gets a share, and the shares sum to the grade. uv run minidraw-exploit-by-reach --release tabular bins the 6.2M spots by how often self-play reaches them (figures/rung3/exploit_by_reach.json, which also checks the sum):

  • Per visit, a table row rots as the sampler sees it less. The commonest band leaks 0.4 chips per hundred visits, a spot reached once in ten million hands about 20, and the rarest band about 67. 72% of the total sits in round 2.
  • A net leaks the other way. On the two Deep CFR averages (figures/rung4/exploit_by_reach_deep-*.json) the net leaks about three times the table per visit at the commonest spots and a fifth of it at the rarest. A table is exact where it has samples and bleeds where it doesn't. A net fills the rare rows from the common ones, and pays for it in the common rows.

Monte Carlo CFR, external sampling and lockstep

How rung 3 keeps CFR's walk but samples the deck and the opponent, why the regret weight becomes exactly 1, and how W workers share one table.

Vanilla CFR walks the whole tree on every iteration. Leduc's tree is small enough for that. Mini-drawmaha's has 85.1 billion decision nodes, so a full walk isn't slow, it's impossible. Monte Carlo CFR (MCCFR) keeps the walk and samples the parts of it that are expensive to enumerate. Each iteration then costs one sampled hand instead of the whole game, and the expected update is still CFR's. The repo uses external sampling (mccfr.py), parallelised as lockstep (lockstep.py).

External sampling: sample what is outside the traverser

One traversal per seat per iteration, P0 then P1, each from a freshly dealt root. The traversing seat is the one whose regrets are updated. Rung 2's recursion stays — terminal, chance and decision branches, utilities indexed by position in legal_actions(), ledgers banked on the way back up — and two branches change:

NodeVanilla CFRExternal sampling
Chance (the deck)average over every outcomepick one outcome by its probability
The opponent's spotenumerate every action, weighted by σpick one action from the opponent's current mix
The traverser's spotenumerate every actionenumerate every action, because regret compares the actions and a comparison needs all of them

The weight is the sample

In vanilla CFR the regret bank is multiplied by π₋ᵢ · π_c, how often the deck and the opponent bring play here. Under sampling, that product is exactly the probability the walk arrives here at all. So the update lands with that frequency instead of being multiplied by that coefficient, and the regret weight at the traverser's spot is exactly 1:

at my spot: R(I, ·) += 1 · ( u − ⟨σ, u⟩ )
at their spot: S(I, ·) += t · σ(I, ·)
  • u[k] is what the traverser's k-th action earned down the sampled path; ⟨σ, u⟩ is what its current mix earned on the same path. Their difference is one unbiased sample of the counterfactual regret.
  • S is banked at the opponent's spots, where the walk samples from σ anyway. Arriving there already happened with the opponent's own reach, so adding σ once per arrival reproduces the own-reach weight. The traverser's own spots bank no strategy: they were enumerated, not played, and counting them would credit every action with having been chosen.
  • t makes the average linear: iteration t's strategy counts t times as much, on the premise that later iterates are better.

Warning

Multiplying π₋ᵢ · π_c in on top applies the probability twice. Nothing crashes. The opponent's reach, which differs across the worlds of one spot because the opponent's cards differ, ends up squared, so the worlds the opponent rarely produces are under-trained and the solve converges somewhere else. On Leduc that walk stalls at 0.14–0.20 exploitability, where the correct one reaches 0.05, and the two look the same for the first few hundred thousand iterations. Pattern-matching leduc/cfr.py is how you'd make this mistake, so traverse has no reach parameter to thread through.

Why the linear average is not free under vanilla regret

On Leduc under vanilla regret, the uniform averaging column beats the linear one at every length: 1.5–2.7× at 10k iterations, and about 4× by a million (0.012 against 0.050). The docstring calls this a measurement, not yet an explanation. It is consistent with vanilla CFR's regret bound applying to the uniform average (linear averaging is backed when the regrets are weighted to match, as in LCFR and CFR+), and with a t-weighted average having a smaller effective sample. Under the other three rules the linear column is the better one. On mini-drawmaha the race found linear beats uniform for both vanilla (8.6 against 15.5 per hundred hands) and CFR+ (24.8 against 36.5). Extra averaging columns cost nothing to keep, because nothing reads an average while training.

Lockstep: W hands per seat against one frozen σₜ

A one-hand walk writes as it goes, so two processes can't share a table. A lockstep iteration walks W hands per seat (one per worker) and writes nothing while it walks:

  1. Freeze σₜ. Each of the W workers walks one sampled hand for seat s and records what it would write.
  2. Once all W are in, every touched row is banked once, with the sum of its workers' regrets, and the strategy mass is added. The rows are banked in a fixed order.
  3. The other seat does the same against the updated regrets (alternating updates).

The W walks of one seat are a phase. This is MCCFR with W samples per iteration. In the race W = 10, so one lockstep iteration is 10 hands per seat, and 2,000,000 iterations is 20M hands per seat.

Why lockstep is the right shape

  • No races. The table is read-only during a phase, so the walks can't race. The one write per row happens in a fixed order, so the float sums are the same however the walks were scheduled. parallel.py runs the W walks in W processes; lockstep.py runs them one after another and is the reference the processes must equal bit for bit.
  • Every regret rule is exact. CFR+ floors a row once per iteration, on the total of its W sampled regrets. That is the paper's rule with that total as the iteration's regret, not W separately floored one-hand banks.
  • Textbook semantics. A walk that reaches the same row twice (suit relabelling can do that) reads σₜ both times, instead of seeing its own first bank the second time.

Counter-based randomness makes resume exact

Worker w's walk for seat s at iteration t draws from default_rng([seed, t, s, w]). A run carries no RNG state, so resuming from any checkpoint is the same as never having stopped. Deep CFR inherits the rule: every draw is a function of (seed, t, seat, walk index), never of W, so arms with different worker counts see identical hands.

The memory: 6.2M ledgers in four flat arrays

6,220,050 infosets, each a RegretMatcher 2–4 wide, would be millions of Python objects. packed_table.py stores them as four flat arrays — the ledger contract's cumulative_regret, strategy_sum, extra_sums, stamp — and hands out ledgers as views into them, which is why ledger writers update in place and never rebind.

Regret rules — why linear weighting won, twice

Vanilla, LCFR, CFR+ and DCFR differ only in what a visit banks. Under sampling, the two rules that test a regret's sign lost to linear weighting, tabular and neural alike.

Once the walk has measured one sample of counterfactual regret, r = u − ⟨σ, u⟩, at one of the traverser's spots, something has to decide how r enters the stored total. That decision is the regret rule, and it is the only thing that differs between the four algorithms the repo raced. In the code a rule is a function bank(ledger, r, t) and nothing else (regret_rules.py). The average isn't the rule's business: every rule banks the same played strategies into the same averages.

The headline: on mini-drawmaha the two rules designed to beat linear CFR lost to it by 3–4× tabular and by about 17× neural. Both times the cause was the same. A rule that depends on a regret's sign, or runs on its own clock, stops meaning what its paper meant once rows are sampled. LCFR survives because its weight w(t) = t depends on neither. Linear weighting is the repo's default for anything sampled.

The four rules, stated on a running total R

DCFR discounted lazily, and why that is exact

As written in the paper, DCFR discounts every regret of the traverser on every iteration, visited or not. Under sampling a row is visited once in thousands of iterations, and touching all 6.2M rows per iteration is out of the question. So a row is discounted only when it is next visited, with all the owed iterations at once. That is exact because an unvisited row receives r = 0, and multiplying by a positive factor never changes a sign. Each entry stays positive or negative over the whole gap, so the per-sign factors simply multiply:

positive entries: ∏ₖ k^1.5 / (k^1.5 + 1) = exp( L(t−1) − L(s−1) )
negative entries: (½)^(t − s)

s is the row's stamp, the last iteration it was brought up to. L is the running log of the positive factor. The lazy rule equals the eager one bit for bit.

DCFR collapsed onto CFR+ because of its clock

The implementation is correct. The problem is what "per iteration" means once rows are sampled. A row visited once every g iterations pays g iterations of discount between two pieces of evidence:

negative regret at the next visit = (½)^g × what was banked
  • g, the gap between visits, was large. At 2M iterations the median row was last visited about 37,000 iterations earlier (the reach-weighted median gap was 7,006).
  • Float64 reaches exactly zero after about 1,075 halvings. So every negative regret was gone before its row came round again, which is exactly CFR+'s floor.
  • The positive side failed too. DCFR's positive discount does its work in the first few dozen ticks of the global clock, and 73% of rows were first reached after that window, so their positive regret was never discounted.

In the limit of rare visits DCFR(α, β, γ) → DCFR(∞, −∞, γ), which Brown & Sandholm identify with CFR+. Both runs used the same linear average, and they were within 0.7% at every checkpoint. They aren't identical, though (24.8 against 25.0): DCFR also discounts positive regret.

LCFR was immune for two reasons

  • Sign-blind. Weighting by t treats positive and negative regret alike, so idle time can't tilt the balance between an action's good and bad history.
  • Scale-free. w(t) = t has no built-in time scale. A rare row's updates arrive at t ≈ j/p, and the ratio of two of its weights is tᵢ/tⱼ ≈ i/j whatever the visit rate p is. A rare row gets the same linear-in-evidence weighting as a frequent one.

Note

The rule that follows: decaying untouched rows is harmless exactly when the decay is sign-blind and scale-free. DCFR's discount is neither. CFR+'s floor has no clock, so it is still CFR+ under sampling. Why CFR+ itself lost to LCFR by 3–4× was not isolated. One untested guess: a floored total forgets an action's bad history, and sampling noise is what makes that history worth keeping. LCFR against vanilla (6.1 against 8.6) is one seed, so suggestive, not settled.

Rung 4, neural: per-sample DCFR and CFR+ lose by about 17×

Deep CFR stores no running regret. Each refit learns the t-weighted mean of the regret samples in the reservoir, and regret matching reads that mean's sign pattern. So the only place a rule can act is on the individual samples. The screen (train.rule_targets, PR #62) rescales each stored sample by the weight the rule would give it at iteration now, using that sample's own sign:

DCFR, positive sample from iteration s: ∏ₖ₌ₛ^(now−1) k^1.5 / (k^1.5 + 1)
DCFR, negative sample from iteration s: (½)^(now − s)
CFR+: positive samples keep weight 1; negative samples count only when s = now

A sample's sign is not its cell's sign

figures/rung4/rule_sign_flips.json measures the mechanism. By t = 50 the screen keeps only 4.1% (DCFR) or 2.1% (CFR+) of the memory's negative regret mass, against 82–100% of its positive mass. Once the negatives are discounted away and the lucky positives aren't, the fitted mean turns positive. 36–38% of seat 0's cells that are negative under linear weights turn positive by t = 50, up from 8–11% at t = 10. Regret matching plays every positive cell, so these are bad actions the net is being taught to play, and there are more of them every iteration. That fits the rising curve, but no ablation has isolated it as the only cause.

A worked case shows the shape. Two actions' samples: b = {+1, −3, +1, −3}, bad on average at −1 per sample, and c = {+0.5, +0.5, +0.5, +0.5}, mildly good. Clip per sample and both fit to +0.5, so regret matching plays them equally. CFR+ on the totals would floor b at zero and play only c. A per-sample rule inflates the noisy action, and sampling produces noisy actions.

Deep CFR — the regret table replaced by a network

Why full Drawmaha needs a net instead of a table, how rung 4 swaps one in without touching the walk or the grader, and what it has measured so far.

A table needs one row per infoset. Mini-drawmaha's 6.2M rows fit in memory; full Drawmaha's don't come close. Deep CFR (Brown, Lerer, Gross & Sandholm 2019) runs CFR with the regret table replaced by a neural network that predicts each spot's regrets from features of the spot. Rung 4 (deepcfr/) puts it on mini-drawmaha, a game where the exact answer and an exact grader already exist. The cost of the net can then be read off at every point:

price = exploitability(Deep CFR's average) ÷ exploitability(LCFR's linear average), at equal hands per seat
  • The numerator is graded by the same exact meter as rung 3 (see Exploitability).
  • The denominator is the rung-3 race's LCFR at the same number of sampled hands.
  • Equal hands: Deep CFR at K = 10,000 walks per seat per iteration has seen at iteration t the same hands LCFR had at lockstep iteration 1,000·t (LCFR's W = 10). So the eight marks t = 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 500, 1,000, 2,000 land exactly on the race's 100k–20M checkpoints.
  • There is no pass mark. The ratio is recorded and read.

One iteration is rung 3's lockstep with the regret half swapped out

For each seat in turn (train.py):

  1. Materialise. Write both seats' nets into the packed table's regret slots (materialise.py). The walk then plays regret matching over the table as before, with RegretMatcher.strategy() unchanged.
  2. Average. Let the exact average see the iteration's strategy while it sits in the table (average.py).
  3. Walk. K hands on W processes (collect.py), returning the traverser's regret samples and the opponent's strategy samples as rows.
  4. Remember. Merge the rows into fixed-size reservoirs, 50M rows each (reservoir.py).
  5. Refit from scratch. Throw the seat's net away and fit a fresh one to its whole memory (fit.py).

Materialise writes eq. (3) into the slots

Deep CFR plays regret matching over the net's outputs when any output is positive, and the single best action when none is (eq. 3 of the paper). Plain regret matching would read all-negative as uniform, so materialise writes the verdict in a form the ledger already reads correctly:

Net outputs at a spotWritten to the slotsThe ledger then plays

all equal (an untrained net, an exact tie, no net)zerosuniform

It is 141 batched forward passes, one per public point, each written with one slice assignment.

The net and its loss

The net is a plain MLP (net.py): 136 input features describing what the actor can see, then hidden layers, then 7 signed outputs, one per action in a fixed order, so a column means the same thing at every spot. The head is linear because the outputs stand in for signed regrets; a ReLU or softmax would throw away the sign materialise needs. The default is 3 × 64 (17,543 parameters); the graded arms went to 3 × 128 and 4 × 256.

The refit minimises a t-weighted squared error:

loss = mean over rows of ( t / mean t ) · Σ_legal a ( net(I)[a] − target[a] )²
  • target is a sampled regret r = u − ⟨σ, u⟩ at row I's legal columns. Padding columns are masked out before the subtraction.
  • t / mean t is Linear CFR's weight carried into the fit: early iterations' regrets were sampled against near-random strategies and say the least about the game. Dividing by the batch's mean t keeps the loss on one scale as the run lengthens.
  • Squared error means the net learns the t-weighted mean regret of each spot, which is what regret matching needs.
  • Adam, gradient norm clipped at 1.0, rows drawn uniformly with replacement from the memory.
Why a reservoir, and why Algorithm R

Over 2,000 iterations the walks offer about 280M advantage rows per seat, and the memory keeps C = 50M. The kept rows should be a uniform sample of everything offered, so no iteration is favoured by when it happened. Algorithm R does that in one pass: the first C offers fill the slots, and offer g ≥ C draws j uniformly from [0, g] and overwrites slot j if j < C. By induction every offer is resident with probability C/(g+1). The draws come from a counter-based stream seeded by (seed, t, seat, memory), and the offer count n is saved with the memory, so a resumed run merges exactly as the uninterrupted one would. A row is 24 bytes: row (int32), t (int32), four float32 targets.

Three averages are graded, because they answer different questions

CFR's guarantee is about the average strategy, and Deep CFR throws the table away every iteration, so nothing keeps the average for free. Rung 4 keeps it two ways and grades both, plus the current iterate:

ObjectWhat it isWhy grade it
Exact averageΣₜ t · own_reachₜ(I) · σₜ(I, a), summed per column while each iteration's strategy sits in the table (Single Deep CFR)CFR's actual answer: no sampling or fitting error in the averaging itself

The exact average's weight is the textbook own reach: the sum, over the histories a key stands for, of seat q's own action probabilities on the way there. The opponent's probabilities are left out because the average is of q's play, not of how the opponent happened to be playing. A post-draw key has three pre-draw predecessors, all of which count, so own reach is pushed through a predecessor table over the 141 public points.

Π gets the same weighting by regression. At a spot with memory rows σ₁, σ₂, σ₃ at weights t₁, t₂, t₃, the output y minimising Σ tᵢ·(y − σᵢ)² is Σ tᵢσᵢ / Σ tᵢ, the t-weighted mean, even though no single row held it. Reach is carried by the sample counts, since walks visit a spot in proportion to how often play arrives. Π carries two errors the exact average doesn't: the reservoir's sampling and the fit's. The exact average beat Π at every graded mark of every linear arm.

Dividing each target by the pot spreads the fit's accuracy by chip cost

One net fits every spot with one accuracy budget, and the loss decides where it goes. A regret is in chips, so a spot's regrets are about as large as its pot, and a relative error ε there costs (ε · pot)² per sample. Raw squared error therefore weighs a spot by how often × pot². The 52-chip spots count 676 times the 2-chip spots they are reached as often as, and the ante spots, where every hand begins, are fitted last.

Divide every target at a spot by pot^k before the fit:

  • It's free for the regrets themselves. The divisor is shared by every action and every sample at the spot, so the spot's mean regret is scaled by one constant. Regret matching reads only signs and ratios, so it plays the same σ from scaled regrets as from raw ones (pot_scaled in train.py; a test pins the invariance). Only the net's errors, and so the σ it actually plays, move.
  • What k does. The weight left is how often × pot^(2 − 2k). k = ½ weighs by how often × pot, the chips a σ mistake at that spot actually costs, and is the default. k = 1 weighs every spot alike.

Measured on one seed, 4 × 256 at 16,000 steps and lr 5e-4: at 2M hands raw 0.115, ÷ √pot 0.086, ÷ pot 0.084 chips/hand. The two pot weightings are tied within the noise.

Warning

One confound is unresolved. fit.py doesn't normalise its targets, so dividing by up to √52 ≈ 7.2 also shrinks the gradients that the 1.0 clip sees. Part of the gain may come from a different clipping regime rather than the reweighting. A raw arm with a looser clip would separate the two, and it hasn't been run.

Abstraction — how mini-drawmaha shrinks full Drawmaha

Why the repo solves a smaller game first, what was cut and what was kept, and the one lossless collapse it allows.

Every CFR-family solver needs something per information set, and real poker has astronomically many. The literature's bridge is abstraction: build a smaller game whose solution says something about the big one. Mini-drawmaha is this repo's abstraction. It is not a bucketing of full Drawmaha's hands. It is a separate, smaller game that keeps every idea full Drawmaha adds over Leduc. It's small enough to solve exactly, so a method can be graded on it before it meets a game where no grade is possible.

What survives the shrink is the list of things Leduc does not have, each of which breaks a naive solver differently:

  • The chance node depends on the action. Stand pat and the deck never acts; throw a card and it deals one. A throw is two nodes, the decision and then the replacement.
  • Thrown cards leave the deck for good, and only their owner knows which. remaining_deck() is derived from the state, never counted down, so a discard can never be redealt.
  • The pot pays twice, by two rules over the same cards. Dealt trips is the inner half's top category and can bring only a pair outside. Strength is two-dimensional.
  • Suits carry information. Both halves have flushes, so Leduc's trick of throwing suits away is no longer lossless.

Every size number was chosen for a reason

  • Three suits because with two, a rank holds two cards and the inner half loses trips entirely.
  • Five ranks because at four, a run of four distinct ranks is the whole rank set, and high card and plain flush would have nowhere to exist.
  • 26-chip stacks because from the 2-chip ante pot an uncapped pot-raise war commits 2 → 8 → 26. The stack buys a bet, a raise and an all-in shove (truncated to the 25 left behind the ante), and nothing deeper. Round 1 then has 13 complete lines, and round 2 opens in one of four (pot, stacks) states, so the infoset key needs no explicit chip counts.
  • Hand rankings by measured rarity in this deck, not 52-card convention. A four-card straight (25.11%) ranks below two pair (16.38%) and trips (9.89%), because a run of four out of five ranks is easy to make. Outer high card is the rarest outer result (0.74%) and still ranks last, by definition.

Note

Mini-drawmaha has no referee. No engine plays Drawmaha, so every rule is a decision, argued in the rung-3 plan (kept outside the repo, ~/Desktop/Claude/poker/rung3-delta/rung3-delta-plan.md, §3–§4). The ground truth is the exact best-response walk. Changing the deck, the draw cap, the betting tree or the infoset key means re-reading that plan first.

The one collapse allowed is relabelling suits, jointly

Within the game, the only abstraction the solver uses is lossless: two positions that differ only in which physical suit is which are the same position. canonical() relabels the three suits to the lexicographically smallest image of the whole visible picture. That takes the 455 dealt hands down to 95 classes, a 4.79× saving that costs nothing.

Warning

"Jointly" is the correctness trap. Canonicalising the hole and the board separately forgets whether they share a suit, which is exactly what a flush is. Two genuinely different positions would hash to one key, and the solve would be quietly wrong rather than loudly broken. The draw adds a second trap: a throw action names a card by position ("throw the lowest"), and the positions are ordered by the canonical labels, so "throw the lowest" means the same card in every deal that shares an infoset.

The same lossless step scales to the target: the survey's notes brute-force full Drawmaha's 2,598,960 five-card hands down to exactly 134,459 canonical classes (19.3×) (docs/research/05-abstraction-bucketing.md).

Counting infosets by factorising the key

85 billion nodes can't be walked to count infosets, but an infoset factorises into two independent halves:

infoset = public point × private key
6,220,050 = Σ over the 141 public points of (private keys at that point's shape)
  • A public point is the betting so far, both draw counts, how many board cards are out, and whose turn it is. There are 141, and none depends on a card, so one representative deal walks them all.
  • A private key is the actor's own (hole, discarded, board) after the joint relabelling, with the board kept in the order it came. There are 970 or 10,170 with one board card out (by whether the actor has thrown), and 10,170 or 100,400 with two.

Keeping the board's order doubles the round-2 half: a sorted board would have given 3,142,290 infosets, merging histories the player can tell apart. The census splits 3,106,630 for P0 and 3,113,420 for P1, and tests/minidrawmaha/census.json holds it.

Two warnings carry over. Refining an abstraction can increase real-game exploitability (Waugh et al. 2009), so an abstraction is validated by exploitability or head-to-head, never assumed. And Drawmaha's split pot makes strength two-dimensional, so a scalar equity is worse than usual.

Deep CFR claims to "obviate the need for abstraction". The survey's synthesis is narrower: nets replace the clustering, not the representation. The net's input is itself an abstraction. Rung 4's 136 features are four card groups of 23 floats each (multi-hot over the deck, plus rank counts and suit counts, a hand-built stand-in for learned card embeddings), plus 44 floats of public state. Two keys that share everything the player can see share a vector, and nothing in the vector names the table row. A net handed key ids would just be a table with extra steps.

The regret ledger

RegretMatcher, the one learning rule every game in the repo trains, and why its two weights are keyword-only.

Every solver in the repo, from rock-paper-scissors to Deep CFR, learns through the same small class: RegretMatcher in regret_matching.py. One ledger sits at each decision point a player can face. It remembers how much better each action would have done than the mix actually played, and it turns that memory into the next strategy. The games around it grow by eight orders of magnitude, and this class barely changes.

What one ledger holds: two vectors, nothing derived

A ledger over n actions stores two persistent vectors and two extra slots:

SlotTypeWhat it is
cumulative_regretfloat64, nR(a): accumulated regret for each action
strategy_sumfloat64, nS(a): the banked strategy mass, the thing that becomes the answer
extra_sumsfloat64, k × nk further averages of the same strategies under other weightings (uniform, quadratic), banked beside the primary one; k = 0 unless a run asks
stampint64, 1the last iteration a lazily applied discount has been brought up to (only DCFR uses it)

The current strategy and the average strategy are never stored. Both are read off these vectors when asked for, so nothing inside a ledger is ever "already averaged".

The three methods

  • strategy(): what this spot plays right now. It keeps only the positive regrets and normalises them, so each action is played in proportion to how much it has been regretted. When no regret is positive, it plays uniformly.
σ(a) = R⁺(a) / Σₐ′ R⁺(a′), with R⁺(a) = max(R(a), 0)
  • average_strategy(): the answer. It normalises strategy_sum, and plays uniformly if nothing was ever banked. In two-player zero-sum games this average converges to equilibrium, while the current strategy cycles forever. Regret matching shows both behaviours on rock-paper-scissors.
  • update(utilities, *, regret_weight, strategy_weight): record one visit. utilities[a] is what action a would have earned. It is read off a payoff matrix in rock-paper-scissors and computed by a subtree walk in the poker games.
R += regret_weight · (u − ⟨σ, u⟩)
S += strategy_weight · σ

Read the first line as follows. ⟨σ, u⟩ is what the mix actually earned, so u − ⟨σ, u⟩ is how much better each pure action would have done. Positive entries are regrets, and negative ones say the action did worse than the mix. The second line adds the strategy that was played into the running sum the average is read from.

update validates its input before it mutates anything. A wrong-width vector, a NaN utility or a negative weight is refused and leaves the ledger exactly as it was. A NaN in particular would poison both accumulators for good, and every later strategy() would quietly return NaN.

Why the two weights are keyword-only

In rock-paper-scissors every round counts the same, so both weights default to 1. In a game tree a spot is only reached some of the time, and the two accumulators want different scales:

WeightIsWhy that scale
regret_weightthe counterfactual reach π₋ᵢ: how often chance and the opponent bring play here, excluding your own choicesa line you currently avoid must keep learning at full strength, or it can never be rediscovered
strategy_weightyour own reach πᵢ (times t in linear averaging)the average has to reproduce what you actually played

Pass them swapped and every game still converges, quietly, to the wrong answer. Kuhn with the weights transposed reports a converged-looking grid that folds the king to a bet 18% of the time. For that reason both weights are keyword-only, and the root AGENTS.md forbids making them positional. The derivation of the two reaches is in CFR.

Warning

Never make regret_weight or strategy_weight positional. A call site that transposes them runs without an error and produces a wrong solve; the only defence is that the transposition cannot be written by accident.

over: a ledger that is only a window

At mini-drawmaha's 6.2 million infosets one Python object per ledger cost 3.4 GB, rising to 5.4 GB once the two extra slots were added. RegretMatcher.over(...) builds a ledger whose four arrays are views into somebody else's flat buffers, with no copy. That somebody is the packed table. An update therefore lands in the table itself, and the ledger object can be thrown away after the visit. over checks every view's shape and dtype, because a wrong-width view or a float32 buffer would not crash: it would bank into a neighbour's columns or lose precision without a word.

That is also why every writer changes the arrays in place (+=, [:] =) and never rebinds an attribute. Rebinding would silently cut the ledger loose from its table. Mini-drawmaha solver covers the packed table this serves.

Rock-paper-scissors

The ledger with nothing around it — the rules, the players, the two experiments and the terminal game in src/drawmaha_solver/rps/.

Rock-paper-scissors is the smallest game with a mixed-strategy equilibrium, the uniform (⅓, ⅓, ⅓). The regret ledger therefore has an exact answer to converge to before any game tree exists. There is no tree, no chance and no hidden information here, only the ledger playing itself. That isolates the one thing every later game inherits: regret matching's average converges and its current strategy does not.

Kuhn poker

The first game tree — twelve ledgers, vanilla CFR's two-direction walk, an exact meter over 64 pure strategies, and an exploit finder built from one extra parameter.

Kuhn poker is the first game with a tree, and so the first where the ledger's utilities have to be computed. The deck has three cards, J < Q < K. Both players ante 1, each gets one card, and one round of betting follows with a single bet size of 1. The whole state of a solve is 12 ledgers of 4 numbers each. Its equilibrium has a closed form, so every number the solver produces can be checked on paper.

The rules: four decisions, two actions, twelve infosets

game.py gives a solver its vocabulary:

  • Action is PASS or BET at every node, so a ledger is always two wide. action_label renders the poker word for display: PASS is a check with nothing to call and a fold facing a bet, and BET is a bet or a call.
  • KuhnState is one node of the tree: the deal plus the actions so far. It is frozen, so apply returns a new node and a walker never has to undo a move. returns pays in chips with the ante as 1 and sums to zero.
  • InfoSet is what the player to act knows: their own card and the public history, and deliberately not the opponent's card. Two deals that differ only in the opponent's card must collapse to one equal, equally hashing key.
  • all_infosets lists the 12 = 4 decision nodes × 3 cards. DEALS lists the six equally likely deals; chance acts only once, at the root.

The memory: a 4 × 3 grid of ledgers

new_infoset_table builds a dict[InfoSet, RegretMatcher] with all twelve entries, so no code path can invent a thirteenth. current_strategy and average_strategy read the whole table at once. format_strategy_grid prints P(BET) as the 4 × 3 grid, with one row per decision node and one column per card. Moving down a column is the same hand in a different spot. Moving across a row is the same spot with a different hand, which is exactly what the opponent cannot see.

The walk: reach goes down, values come up

cfr.py is the only new idea of the game. walk recurses through the tree carrying two quantities in opposite directions:

  • Down: reach. reach[p] is the product of player p's own action probabilities along the path, and nothing else.
  • Up: values. What the node is worth to each seat, as a plain conditional expectation with no reach weighting.

The weights are applied once, when a ledger is banked: regret at the counterfactual reach, the average at the acting player's own reach (see The regret ledger). walk returns the value to both seats, so nothing is ever negated on the way up. run_iteration walks the tree once per deal. train repeats that for the requested number of iterations and returns the table. The full derivation is in CFR.

Note

There is no seed. Vanilla CFR enumerates the whole tree and never samples, so two runs agree bit for bit, and the committed figures/rung1/ doubles as a regression fingerprint for cfr.py. A change that moves any number shows up as a dirty working tree.

The meter: all 64 pure strategies, exactly

exploitability.py measures what a perfect adversary wins, (BR₀ + BR₁) / 2, which is zero exactly at Nash. Each player owns 6 infosets with 2 actions, so best_response_value enumerates all 2⁶ = 64 pure strategies for the responder and keeps the best one. Mixed play is handled by weighting both branches at every node, and the hidden card by enumerating all six deals at ⅙, so every number is an exact expectation. best_response returns the counter-strategy itself, expected_value is what each seat earns when both play a given profile, and exploitability averages the two best responses.

Enumerating pure strategies over infosets is what keeps the adversary honest. A pure strategy fixes one action per infoset before any card is dealt, so an opponent who sees your card cannot be written down. A test pins that a "peeking" meter would score the true equilibrium at 0.278 chips a hand.

The exploit finder: CFR against something that cannot move

exploiter.py asks the opposite question, "what beats this?", with no new algorithm. LockedStrategy pins some infosets to probabilities you supply. They never bank an update, but their probabilities still drive the reach, so they still enter the learner's counterfactual weight. Minimising regret against a player who cannot move back converges to a best response instead of to Nash. exploit runs that training and samples Checkpoints along the way. ExploitResult carries the grade: gap is how far CFR still is from the exact ceiling the meter computes. locked_seat tells the two cases apart. A whole locked seat has a ground-truth ceiling. A partial lock leaves the rest of that seat learning, which gives a constrained equilibrium rather than a best response, so no ceiling is claimed.

Leduc poker

Chance in the middle of the tree, ledgers of varying width, a best-response recursion that replaces enumeration, and OpenSpiel as a frozen referee.

Leduc poker has six cards, two each of J, Q and K. Each player gets one, a betting round follows, then one public board card, then a second betting round at double the stake. Two things change from Kuhn, and between them they are the whole game. The deck now acts in the middle of the hand, so the walk meets a chance node mid-tree. And spots no longer all offer the same actions, so ledgers are two or three wide depending on where they sit. Leduc has 288 infosets against Kuhn's 12, and its equilibrium has no closed form, so ground truth comes from an outside implementation.

The rules: three actions, never three choices

  • Action is FOLD, CALL or RAISE, but legal_actions_for makes folding illegal with nothing to call and raising illegal once the round's two-raise cap is spent. A node is therefore 2 or 3 wide, and legal_actions() is the authority on which.
  • LeducState is one node: deal, board, both rounds' betting. kind reads which of the three things it is (NodeKind: decision, chance or terminal). The walk gains a chance branch

cfr.py's walk now has three branches, and the chance branch does two different things:

  • For values, it averages. Nobody chooses the board, so a chance node's value is the plain probability-weighted average of its children. Every round-1 regret above it depends on this average.
  • For weights, it multiplies a receipt. Counterfactual reach includes chance, so the walk threads π_c down: 1/30 once the private cards are out, and 1/120 once the board lands. It rides in the regret weight only. The strategy weight is still the player's own reach.

One iteration walks 9,450 nodes in about 50 ms, so leduc-analysis defaults to 2,000 iterations, roughly two minutes. As with Kuhn there is no seed, and figures/rung2/ is a regression fingerprint.

The meter: sum over hidden worlds first, then take one max

A Leduc seat has about 6 × 10⁵¹ pure strategies, so Kuhn's enumeration is dead. best_response_value runs the recursion that was hiding inside the enumeration:

BRᵢ(I) = maxₐ Σ_{h ∈ I} π_c(h) · π₋ᵢ(h) · v(h · a)

Each piece is a part of the problem. I is the responder's infoset. h ranges over the hidden worlds it could be, which are the opponent's possible cards. π_c(h) · π₋ᵢ(h) is how often the deck and the frozen opponent produce that world. v(h · a) is what action a then earns there. The order is the point. The sum over worlds happens inside and the max outside, because one action has to be chosen for the whole infoset. Put the max inside and it picks a different action per world: that is an adversary who peeks at face-down cards, and it over-reports exploitability. best_response returns the counter-strategy, and expected_value and exploitability are as in Kuhn. See Exploitability.

Mini-drawmaha rules

The 15-card bomb pot with one draw and a split pot — the deck and its suit relabelling, both hand rankings, the state machine, the 6,220,050-infoset enumeration and the 136-float features the nets read.

Mini-drawmaha is a shrunken Drawmaha that keeps the two things the full game is made of, a draw and a split pot, on a deck small enough that the exact answer can still be computed. It is the last game where "how far is this strategy from optimal?" has an exact answer, which is why Deep CFR is graded here before it meets the real game. No engine implements Drawmaha, so every rule is a decision, and rules/ has no outside referee. It holds no solver code either: the exact grader imports this package and nothing else.

The hand, start to finish

  1. Both players ante 1 from a 26-chip stack. It is a bomb pot: no blinds, no position asymmetry and no preflop betting.
  2. Three private cards each, then board card 1, face up.
  3. Betting round 1. Pot-limit, so the actions are fold, check/call and pot, with the bet capped at the stack.
  4. The draw. P0 throws at most one card, then P1 does, knowing whether P0 drew but never what. The replacement is dealt face down; the full game's face-up draw-one rule is not played here.
  5. Board card 2, then betting round 2, then the showdown.
  6. The pot splits in half. The inner half goes to the best 3-card hand made of the three private cards alone. The outer half goes to the best 4-card hand made of exactly two private cards plus both board cards. Winning both halves is a scoop.

cards.py: five ranks, three suits, one relabelling

Card is a rank 0–4 (printed 2–6) and a suit 0–2. Three suits is forced: with two, a rank holds only two cards and the inner half loses trips. The fifth rank gives the outer half a complete ranking. hand_symbol and parse_cards convert to and from text such as "6h 2c".

Suits carry information here, because both halves of the pot have flushes. The table may forget which physical suit is which, but never how the suits relate. canonical relabels the three suits to the lexicographically smallest image of all the visible groups jointly, and canonical_relabelling returns the relabelling it used. relabel applies one. Doing the hole and the board separately would forget whether they share a suit, which is exactly what a flush is. The joint relabelling takes the 455 possible hands down to 95 classes at no cost.

hands.py: rankings by measured rarity in this deck

inner_score scores the best 3-card hand of the three held cards. outer_score takes the best of the three ways to choose exactly two hole cards and scores each with four_card_score against both board cards. Each returns a Score: the category (InnerCategory, six of them; OuterCategory, seven), then what it is made of, so plain tuple comparison decides who wins.

Both orders are measured rarity in this 15-card deck, not 52-card convention, which makes two rows look wrong when they are correct. A four-card straight (25.11%) ranks below two pair and trips, because a run of four from five ranks is easy to make. Outer high card is the rarest outer result (0.74%) and still ranks last, because high card is the absence of a made hand.

game.py: the state machine

  • Action holds every decision in one enum: FOLD, CHECK_CALL and POT for betting, and four throws (none, low, mid, top) for the draw. Values are not ledger indices. throw_count is the public half of a throw. action_label and line_symbol are for display.
  • MiniState is one node: both holes, the discards, the board and the betting. Its kind (NodeKind) is decision, chance or terminal. The chance node depends on the action: a player who stands pat gets no replacement deal. chance_outcomes and apply_chance turn board cards and deal replacements; legal_actions is betting or the four throws; returns pays half the pot on each hand, using pot_shares.
  • ChipState and chip_state replay the betting to report the pot, each player's contribution and what is owed. betting_legal_actions applies pot-limit.
  • InfoSet is what the actor can see, suits relabelled: canonical_picture of (hole, discarded, board in the order it came). The board order is kept on purpose, because round 1 was played against the first card alone. DrawSignal is what the table learns of an opponent's draw: the count and nothing else. draw_order lays the actor's cards out as low/mid/top so a throw names one card.
  • random_deal deals uniformly over all 100,100 deals.

Warning

The key is still slightly coarser than the real infoset. With a rank held twice, "throw the low one" picks a physical card by the relabelling's tie-break, and about one traversal in a thousand banks the same row twice. The grader therefore takes its max per physical state, never per table key: grouping by key under-reports by about 0.012 chips a hand. Mini-drawmaha solver covers what this does to the regret rules.

enumeration.py: 6,220,050 infosets from two small halves

The concrete tree has 85,129,644,600 decision nodes, so it cannot be walked. A position does factorise into two small, independent halves:

  • A public point (PublicPoint): the betting so far, both draw counts, how many board cards are out and whose turn it is. Its width is the ledger width at that point. public_tree builds the whole public game, 141 decisions, 36 chance nodes and 178 terminals, from one representative deal, because no public point depends on a card. merged combines two routes into the same public spot by adding their weights, and refuses if the arrivals disagree about who acts. public_decision_points lists the 141 in walk order.
  • A private key (private_keys): the canonical (hole, discarded, board), sorted. A decision stands on one of three shapes: 970 keys with one board card out, 10,170 with two out before a throw, and 100,400 with two out after one.

all_infosets is two nested loops over those halves and yields each infoset exactly once, 6,220,050 in all. Streaming them takes about fifteen seconds.

Mini-drawmaha solver

The packed table, external-sampling MCCFR, the four regret rules, lockstep's determinism contract, W processes over shared memory, and the run driver that survives being killed.

Mini-drawmaha has 6.2 million infosets, about 21,000 times Leduc's 288, and a tree of 85 billion decision nodes, so a full pass per iteration does not run at all. solver/ keeps the shape of the earlier walks and changes three things. It stores the ledgers in four flat arrays, samples the deck and the opponent instead of enumerating them, and walks W sampled hands per seat against one frozen strategy so that W processes can share one table. The race this machinery ran, four regret rules for 20M hands per seat on Modal, is what picked LCFR as the tabular answer.

The packed table: 0.3 GB instead of 5.4 GB

The first version held one RegretMatcher object per key: 3.4 GB and half a minute to build. Ten of those cannot share a machine. PackedTable keeps the numbers and drops the objects, in four public arrays laid out in all_infosets() order:

table[key] returns a ledger window (RegretMatcher.over) onto that key's slice. whole_game builds the full table, listed builds one over chosen keys (the tests use it), and over lays the index over buffers someone else owns: shared memory, or a loaded checkpoint. row_of and slot_of locate a key's row and columns. widths, one per row, is the table's fingerprint.

infoset_table.py keeps the familiar interface on top: new_infoset_table, plus ledger_widths, which counts ledgers by width without allocating. A ledger is 2 wide at an open or a shove, 3 facing a bet and 4 at the draw. Column 0 is CHECK_CALL at the open, FOLD facing a bet and THROW_NONE at the draw. A uniform 4-wide table would bank regret for throwing a card at a betting spot, and that phantom column could win the normalisation. External sampling: sample the deck and the opponent, enumerate yourself

mccfr.py keeps Leduc's recursion and changes two branches. A chance node follows one outcome drawn by its probability, and an opponent's spot follows one action drawn from the opponent's current mix. The traverser's own spots are still fully enumerated, because regret is a comparison between actions and a comparison needs all of them. Since the walk arrives at a spot exactly as often as π₋ᵢ · π_c, the sample is the weight:

at my spot: R(I, ·) += 1 · (u − ⟨σ, u⟩)
at their spot: S(I, ·) += t · σ(I, ·)

traverse is that walk. run_iteration deals a fresh hand for P0 to traverse and another for P1, alternating. Solve is everything needed to continue a run (table, t, RNG, rule, columns), with new_solve and train around it, and save_solve / load_solve / read_run for flat .npz checkpoints. State is the nine-member interface the walk reads, which LeducState and MiniState both satisfy, so the same walk was calibrated on Leduc first. See MCCFR.

The four regret rules

regret_rules.py is the only thing that differs between the rules the race compared. Each bank_* function takes one sampled regret r = u − ⟨σ, u⟩:

RuleBankIn plain words
vanilla (bank_vanilla)R += revery iteration counts the same
LCFR (bank_linear)R += t · riteration t counts t times iteration 1, so noisy early iterations fade
CFR+ (bank_cfr_plus)R ← max(R + r, 0)negative regret is floored at zero, so a late-good action is played at once
DCFR (bank_discounted)pay the discounts owed since the stamp, then addpositive and negative regret are discounted at different rates

Under sampling a row can go 37,000 iterations between visits, so DCFR's per-iteration discount cannot be applied eagerly. The ledger's stamp records how far the discount has been paid, settled_regret reads a row's regret as of iteration t without touching it, and cumulative_log_discount keeps the product in log space. bank_rows banks many rows at once for the packed path. RegretRule and Average name the rules and averaging weights (1, t or t²) a checkpoint records. validate_averages, extra_weights and column_average handle the extra columns, which cost nothing and cannot change play. The mechanism behind why CFR+ and DCFR lost is in Regret rules.

Note

A traverser row can be banked twice in one traversal, through the suit relabelling, so "at most once per iteration" is false here. DCFR therefore defers its discount to the row's next visit, and CFR+ floors after every bank. Do not "simplify" either to the textbook once-per-iteration form.

Lockstep: W walks against one frozen σₜ

lockstep.py makes one iteration W sampled hands per seat, one per worker, all reading the same σₜ and writing nothing while they walk. walk returns a Recorded, the writes it would have made. apply then banks each touched row once, in worker order, with the sum of its regrets. The W walks of one seat are a phase, and seats alternate. Two consequences matter:

  • It is exact MCCFR with W samples per iteration. CFR+ floors once per iteration, on the W-sample total.
  • It is a determinism contract. stream derives each walk's random stream from (seed, t, seat, k), never from W or scheduling. A run's floats are therefore the same however the walks were run.

LockstepSolve, new_lockstep and train_lockstep wrap a run. Flat, flatten and apply_arrays are the array form a worker hands back, banked vectorised. save_lockstep writes atomically, load_lockstep resumes, and is_lockstep / read_lockstep inspect a checkpoint without loading it.

parallel.py: W processes, no locks

ParallelLockstep runs each walk in its own process over one packed table in shared memory (packed_table_for builds it). One phase:

  1. The parent writes (t, seat) into a control block and posts each worker's go semaphore.
  2. Each worker walks its hand, reading σₜ and writing nothing, copies its record into its own scratch block and posts done.
  3. Once done has been taken W times, the parent banks the W records in worker order while every worker waits.

The table is only written while nobody reads it, so no lock is needed. Semaphores are used rather than multiprocessing.Barrier, whose crossing cost about 2 ms on macOS, more than a walk. test_parallel.py holds train and step equal to lockstep bit for bit at the same W.

lockstep_run.py: a run that survives being killed

run_to(out, rule=..., target=..., workers=...) keeps everything in out/: resume.npz, rewritten every few minutes; iter-<t>.npz at each grid mark (kept_name), never overwritten; and progress.jsonl, one line per save. Each save is a temp file renamed into place, then its progress line, then one commit (a Modal Volume's commit, or nothing locally). SIGTERM and SIGINT finish the iteration and save. Calling run_to again resumes, and because the streams are counter-based a resumed run equals one that never stopped. A resume refuses a different W or rule, by name.

The meter: vectors over the public tree

exploitability.py asks the same question as Leduc's meter, sum over hidden worlds first and then one max, but it cannot tag 8.5 × 10¹⁰ concrete worlds. It walks the 141-point public tree instead, carrying a vector over the opponent's private states. At each responder point it carries a vector of values over the responder's states:

v[b, r] = Σₕ w[b, h] · M_b[r, h] (a terminal, board b)
v[b, r] = maxₐ vₐ[b, r] (a responder spot)

r is the responder's private state and h the opponent's. w is the reach vector, how often the deck and the frozen opponent bring h here. M_b is the showdown matrix on board b, zero where the hands share a card. minidrawmaha_game compiles the rules into a VectorGame of arrays once: Node, Edge, DrawMap, Level, KeyMap and Showdown are its parts. exploitability, best_response_value and expected_value answer as before. gains_by_infoset splits a seat's exploitability over infosets, together with how often each is reached; this is what minidraw-exploit-by-reach bins. A grade holds about 1.5 GB of showdown tables and takes about two minutes.

Implementation · Deep CFR

Deep CFR package

Eight modules that replace the regret half of mini-drawmaha's 6.2 million ledgers with a network — without changing the walk, the ledger or the grader.

Mini-drawmaha's tabular solve keeps one ledger per infoset. That works for 6.2 million infosets and is impossible for full Drawmaha, whose table could never exist. deepcfr/ replaces the ledgers' regret half with a network that reads what the player can see, the 136 features from rules/features.py, and outputs one number per action. Everything else stays the same: the walk is mini-drawmaha's lockstep.walk, the ledger's strategy() is unchanged, and the exact grader is untouched. Mini-drawmaha's answer is the yardstick. At equal hands per seat, how exploitable is Deep CFR compared with LCFR?

The trick: write the net into the table, don't teach the walk about nets

The walk already plays regret matching over cumulative_regret. So instead of changing the walk, materialise writes each net's outputs into the regret slots before every phase, and the walk plays the net through the ledger it always read. The table becomes a cache of the net's opinion, rewritten twice per iteration.

net.py: 136 → 64 → 64 → 64 → 7, linear head

Net has depth hidden layers of width ReLU units and a linear head of 7, one output per Action in enum order, so an output column means the same thing at every spot. A spot reads its 2, 3 or 4 legal columns through legal_gather; the rest are trained on nothing and read by nobody. The head is linear because the outputs stand in for cumulative regrets, which are signed, and materialise needs the sign. Width and depth are parameters so one trainer can run the size sweep. The default 3 × 64 has 17,543 parameters, a number a test pins. The best arms ran 4 × 256. predict runs chunks of 262,144 rows under inference_mode and returns float32 NumPy. save_net / load_net store the size and weights, and loading uses weights_only=True so a file cannot execute code.

materialise.py: eq. (3) by the write

Deep CFR plays regret matching over the net's outputs when any is positive, and the single best action when none is. The ledger cannot express the second clause, since it reads all-negative as uniform. So regret_slots_from_outputs writes:

materialise does this for every row of the whole game, one pass per public point, because each point's rows are a contiguous slice beside one public feature row.

fit.py: throw the net away, fit a new one

Deep CFR does not update a net between iterations. It fits a fresh one to everything in memory. fit_from_scratch takes three equal-length arrays: rows (which table row; turned into the 136 features, never seen as a number), t (the iteration that offered the sample) and targets (4 wide, padded). batch_loss is per-row squared error over the row's legal columns, with padding masked before the subtraction so a padded NaN never reaches a gradient. Each row is weighted by t over the batch's mean t, borrowed from Linear CFR, so later and better samples outvote early ones. FitConfig is the net's size, Adam's steps, batch, rate, clip, threads and device (cpu or mps). Nothing here knows what a target is, so the same fit serves the regret nets and Π.

reservoir.py: a uniform sample of everything ever offered

Over 2,000 iterations the walks offer about 280 million advantage rows per seat. A memory keeps C = 50 million. Reservoir uses Algorithm R so that every offer is resident with probability C/n and no iteration is favoured by when it happened. The first C offers fill the slots. Offer g ≥ C draws j uniformly from [0, g] and overwrites slot j if j < C. merge offers one phase's rows at once. contents returns read-only (rows, t, targets) for the refit, and size, offered and capacity report the counts. reservoir_stream seeds each merge from (seed, t, seat, Memory), and save / load resume exactly. A row is 24 bytes (int32 row, int32 t, float32 × 4 target), so a memory is 1.2 GB.

collect.py: walks in, memory rows out

Collector owns the run's table and W spawned workers for the whole run. collect runs walks 0..K−1 of one seat at iteration t against the table as it stands. The walk records, per traverser visit, (key, slot, r) with r = u − ⟨σ, u⟩, and per opponent visit the strategy it sampled from. rows_of reshapes one walk's record into a Collected: advantage rows (traverser rows and their r, padded to 4) and policy rows (opponent rows and their σ). joined lays K of them end to end. σ is read from the walk's uniform averaging column, whose weight is t⁰ = 1, so it is σ bit for bit; dividing t · σ by t would not be. Walk k's stream is lockstep.stream(seed, t, seat, k), so the rows never depend on W. A Mac run and a Modal run of one config see identical hands.

Warning

Anything handed to the collector reaches a spawned worker by pickle, so it must be importable by module name in a fresh process: a module-level function, never a lambda or a closure. A learner's write assigns into table.cumulative_regret and never rebinds it, because the workers read that array through a shared block.

average.py: CFR's actual answer, rebuilt exactly

The strategy played at iteration t never settles; only the average converges. The tabular solver got the average for free in strategy_sum, and Deep CFR throws the table away every iteration. ExactAverage rebuilds it (Single Deep CFR). While each iteration's whole strategy sits materialised in the table, accumulate adds

sums(I, a) += t · own_reachₜ(I) · σₜ(I, a)

and frozen normalises each row for the grader. Own reach is the weight because the average strategy must reproduce what the seat actually played, so a spot it rarely steers into counts for little. own_reach pushes reach through the 141 public points using predecessors (a Pred per point) and current_strategy. A post-draw key has three pre-draw predecessors, since the key forgets which card was drawn, and all of them count, with multiplicity. A brute-force walk in tests/deepcfr/test_average.py is the gate. save / load make it part of the resume set, because the sum cannot be rebuilt later except by replaying every net.

policy.py: Π, the average a net can carry

At a game whose table cannot exist, ExactAverage is impossible. The original Deep CFR keeps the average anyway. Every opponent visit copies (row, t, σ) into a policy reservoir, and a fresh net is fit to it with the same t-weighted squared error. The output minimising Σ tᵢ(y − σᵢ)² at a spot is Σ tᵢσᵢ / Σ tᵢ, the t-weighted mean, and the reach weight rides in how often each spot was sampled. fit_policy fits Π at a mark under policy_seed. policy_strategy freezes it over every key: clipped, normalised, uniform if all zero. Π carries two errors the exact average does not, the reservoir's sampling and the fit's. Measuring both on mini-drawmaha is the reason it is graded.

Deep CFR training

One iteration of the loop, the oracle pin that proves it is CFR, the resume state, dividing regret targets by the pot, and how runs are launched on a Mac or on Modal and graded at eight grid marks.

deepcfr/train.py is the order the other seven modules are called in, plus everything a 100-hour run needs around them: a resume state that survives a crash, grid-mark saves, a progress line per iteration and a graceful stop. It adds no new math. This page follows one iteration, then the run around it, then how a run is launched and graded.

One iteration: two phases, each a write, a walk and a refit

Seats alternate, as in lockstep. For each seat p, in order:

for p in (0, 1):
    learner.write(table, nets)                    # both seats' current nets into the regret slots
    for observe in observers: observe(table, t, 1 - p)   # the exact average sees σₜ
    rows = collector.collect(seat=p, t=t, walks=K)        # K walks for seat p
    advantage[p].merge(rows.advantage_rows, rows.advantage_targets, t=t, rng=...)
    policy.merge(rows.policy_rows, rows.policy_targets, t=t, rng=...)
    nets[p] = learner.fit(*advantage[p].contents(), seed=(seed, t, p)).model

The refit is about 95% of the time. Moving it to Apple's GPU (--device mps) cut a 4 × 256, 8,000-step refit from 127 s to 34 s per seat.

The learner seam, and the oracle that proves the loop is CFR

The loop never names torch. It talks to a Learner with four methods, fit, write, save and load. NetLearner is the real one; it imports fit, materialise and net inside those methods, and fit_config builds its FitConfig once torch is first needed. Fitted is what a refit returns, the model and its loss on a held batch.

OracleLearner is a "perfect net". Its model (OracleModel) is a copy of the memory, and its write puts at every row the t-weighted sum of every sample there, in LCFR's own float order. With it, Deep CFR collapses into the tabular solver's LCFR. tests/deepcfr/test_train.py pins the loop bit for bit over three iterations against LCFR built from lockstep's own walk, flatten and apply_arrays, with each sample rounded through float32 first. Every possible join error breaks that pin: a stale table, the wrong t, the wrong seat, a dropped or reordered row. The test runs in the default suite with no torch.

Warning

The oracle pin is the correctness gate. A change to the loop's order, to the float32 cast in collect.rows_of, or to regret_rules.bank_rows breaks it, and if it breaks, the loop is wrong, not the test.

Dividing each regret target by the pot

pot_scaled exists because one net fits every spot's mean regret with a single accuracy budget, and the loss decides where that budget goes. Raw chips spend it badly.

  • What each piece is. A regret is in chips, so a spot's regrets are about the size of its pot. A relative error ε in the net's output there costs (ε · pot)² per sample. Raw squared error therefore weighs a spot by how often it is reached × pot². A 52-chip spot counts 676 times as much as a 2-chip spot reached equally often, and the ante spots where every hand begins are fitted last.
  • Why dividing is free. The divisor is the pot at the decision, which every sample at that spot shares. Each spot's mean regret is scaled by one constant across its actions, and regret matching reads only a row's sign pattern and ratios, so it plays the same σ from scaled regrets as from raw ones. What changes is what the fitted net gets wrong, and so the σ it actually plays. A divisor drawn from the sampled hand's outcome would reweight samples within a spot and move its mean, which is a different algorithm.
  • What the power k does. Dividing by potᵏ leaves a weight of how often × pot^(2 − 2k). k = 0 is raw chips (pot²). k = ½ leaves how often × pot, the chips a σ mistake there actually costs. k = 1 weighs every spot alike, whatever its pot.

spot_pots gives the pot at each of the 141 public points. The default pot_power is ½ since 2026-10-27. Measured on one seed at 2M hands per seat, the exact average was 0.115 raw, 0.086 with ÷ √pot and 0.084 with ÷ pot. Run on to 20M hands, the ÷ pot arm flattens at 0.068. docs/notes/rung4-run-log.md (findings 7 and 8) has the caveats, including a gradient-clipping confound that no arm has separated yet.

rule_targets is the screen behind the DCFR and CFR+ arms. It rescales each sample by what the rule would weigh it at iteration now. Because Deep CFR stores no running total, the sign it tests is each sample's own rather than the cumulative regret's. That is why per-sample DCFR and CFR+ lost to linear by about 17×. See Regret rules.

Paper, ink and one oxblood accent; two type voices; motion read from three tokens; and a test that enforces the mechanical half on every stylesheet.

web/DESIGN.md is the UI standard, and it is enforced. Every surface is held to it: the static pages, the archived Cover, and the five apps. The standard treats taste the way Emil Kowalski describes design engineering, as a set of decisions made the same way every time. Most of them nobody consciously notices, and together they make software feel right.

The standard has two halves. The mechanical half (which curve, how long, what a press does, what reduced motion keeps) is checked by tests/test_ui_standard.py on every stylesheet. The judgement half is a review table at the end of DESIGN.md. Before you touch a stylesheet, a <style> block or a component's classes, read DESIGN.md. Afterwards, run:

uv run pytest tests/test_ui_standard.py tests/test_shared_chrome.py tests/test_cover_page.py

Motion reads three tokens

--ease-out: cubic-bezier(0.23, 1, 0.32, 1);     /* anything entering or settling */
--ease-in-out: cubic-bezier(0.77, 0, 0.175, 1); /* a thing that moves while on screen */
--t-press: 160ms;                               /* the press */

The built-in keywords are too weak to read as intended. ease is kept for exactly one job: a colour, background or border changing under the pointer, at 150 ms. ease-in is never used. It delays the first frame of movement, which is the frame the user is watching, so 300 ms of ease-in feels slower than 300 ms of ease-out.

Before animating anything, decide in this order:

  1. How often will the user see it? A keyboard action, or a bar that updates a thousand times a second, never animates; the number printed beside it would disagree with the bar. A hover gets 150 ms of colour. A sheet opening is occasional and gets the full treatment.
  2. What is it for? Press feedback, state, spatial consistency, or preventing a jarring swap. "It looks cool" is not a purpose for anything seen more than once.
  3. Which curve? Entering or settling uses --ease-out. Moving while on screen uses --ease-in-out. Colour under the pointer uses ease. Constant motion uses linear.
  4. How long? A press is 160 ms, hover colour 150 ms, a popover or sheet 150–250 ms. Nothing in the chrome runs over 300 ms per declaration, delays included. Exits are faster than entrances.

Testing: the suite is the CI

No workflow runs tests; uv run pytest before every PR is the gate, one directory per game, with fixtures re-derived a second way and heavy work gated by environment variables.

CI runs no tests. The only GitHub workflow, .github/workflows/claude-code-review.yml, is an automated code review. The gate is the suite you run yourself:

uv run pytest                      # the whole default suite, about seven minutes on a laptop
uv run pytest -x tests/kuhn        # one game's directory: the fast loop
cd web/<app> && npm test           # vitest, in any app you touched

Run both before you open a PR. The default suite needs no torch, no Modal, no OpenSpiel and no network download, and it has to stay that way. Anyone with uv sync can check the whole project.

Fixtures are re-derived, not trusted

Some ground truth is expensive or comes from outside the repo, so it is computed once and committed:

  • tests/leduc/referee.json comes from OpenSpiel, through scripts/leduc/generate_referee.py.
  • tests/minidrawmaha/census.json comes from enumerating the game's own rules, through scripts/minidrawmaha/generate_census.py. It takes about fifteen seconds and counts all 6.2 million infosets one at a time.

The tests then rebuild every number in the fixture a second, different way and demand that the two agree. A fixture that only asserted itself would keep passing while the solver rotted. For the same reason, fixtures are regenerated by their script and never edited.

The same idea covers numbers quoted elsewhere. minidrawmaha/test_play.py reads figures/rung3/grades.json. minidrawmaha/test_range_export.py reads the committed /solver export. kuhn/test_exploit_server.py recomputes the README's exploit table from the committed solve. deepcfr/test_rung4_quotes.py checks the run log's scoreboard and the README's rung-4 table against figures/rung4/. A re-export changes a test's inputs, never its assertions.

Heavy work is behind a named switch

Tests that would take minutes or gigabytes are skipped by default. Each skip reason names the environment variable that turns it on:

VariableTurns on
MINIDRAWMAHA_FULL_TABLEbuilding the full 6.2M-row table
MINIDRAWMAHA_FULL_GRADEthe exact best-response walk on a full strategy
MINIDRAWMAHA_FULL_CALIBRATIONthe calibration runs
MINIDRAWMAHA_FULL_DEEPDeep CFR end to end

Tests that need torch call pytest.importorskip("torch"). Install it with uv sync --extra deep to run them.

Three gotchas

  • --import-mode=importlib is set in pyproject.toml, because every game has its own test_game.py and identical basenames would collide at collection. So test modules never import one another (shared helpers are restated, and say so), and tests/ holds no __init__.py.
  • Spawned workers unpickle by module name. A test that hands a job to the parallel collector defines that job at module level and puts the repo root on sys.path first, as minidrawmaha/test_parallel.py and deepcfr/test_collect.py do. A lambda or a closure cannot reach a worker process.
  • Convergence thresholds are seeded and justified in a comment beside them. Loosening one to make a test pass is a finding to report, not a fix.

Warning

A test that fails after your change is information about the solver. The repo's whole method is that a wrong answer converges quietly, so the tests are the place it has to be caught. Read the failing assertion's comment before changing anything.

Training runs on a Mac and on Modal

Every trainer is resumable from its output folder; the Modal launchers deploy first, claim each run with a heartbeat, and continue themselves across the 24-hour cap. Only a person launches them.

Two pieces of training need more than a laptop afternoon. One is the mini-drawmaha race: four regret rules, each run to 2M lockstep iterations. The other is Deep CFR's runs: 4 × 256 nets, up to 2,000 iterations of 10,000 walks per seat. Each has a local script for a Mac and a Modal launcher for the same work in the cloud.

Warning

Modal costs money. Every modal run in this repo starts paid containers. They are launched by a person, never by an agent on its own initiative (AGENTS.md, scripts/AGENTS.md).

The local trainers resume from their output folder

caffeinate -i uv run python scripts/minidrawmaha/run_lockstep.py lcfr --out runs/lockstep-lcfr
caffeinate -i uv run --extra deep python scripts/deepcfr/run_deep_cfr.py --out runs/deep-smoke --target 3 --walks 1000
  • --out is the run. Re-running the same command resumes from what is in the folder: resume.npz for lockstep, <out>/resume/ for Deep CFR, the exact average included.
  • Ctrl-C or SIGTERM is safe. The trainer finishes the current iteration and saves.
  • caffeinate -i keeps the Mac awake. Mac sleep still counts toward the training clock, so a run's wall-clock figures include any sleep it suffered.
  • What a resume may change. For Deep CFR, --workers and --device may change on resume. The seed, walks, capacity, net size and --pot-power may not. --device mps is checked against the machine before the run loads, so a missing GPU costs no walks.

The rows a Deep CFR run collects do not depend on the worker count, because collect.py draws every walk from (seed, t, seat, walk). A Mac run and a Modal run of the same config see identical data. Only the refit's arithmetic order can differ.

The Modal launchers share one shape

uv run --with modal modal deploy scripts/deepcfr/modal_rung4.py          # once, and after any code change
uv run --with modal modal run scripts/deepcfr/modal_rung4.py::smoke      # seconds per step, priced
uv run --with modal modal run scripts/deepcfr/modal_rung4.py::train      # the run, resumable
uv run --with modal modal run scripts/deepcfr/modal_rung4.py::status     # where every run stands
uv run --with modal modal run scripts/deepcfr/modal_rung4.py::grade      # every kept mark → figures/rung4/grades.json

scripts/minidrawmaha/modal_rung3.py has the same verbs for the race (smoke, main --target … --rules …, status). Every launcher's docstring opens with its exact commands. The design behind both:

  1. Deploy before you run. modal deploy first, then modal run …. The run spawns on the deployed app, so it outlives your terminal and can spawn its own continuation.
  2. One container trains one run. The race gives each rule a container of 11 cores (10 workers and the parent) and writes to the Volume drawmaha-rung3 under <rule>/ (cfr+ is stored as cfrplus/). Deep CFR gives a run one container of W + 1 cores (32 walk workers and the parent) and writes to drawmaha-rung4 under the run's name, in exactly the layout a Mac run writes: nets/, marks/t00010/ …, resume/ and progress.jsonl.
  3. A heartbeat claims the run. Each trainer writes owner.json at every save. A newcomer refuses to start while another heartbeat is under 15 minutes old, unless that trainer's last progress line says it stopped and saved (a preemption), in which case the newcomer takes over. Two newcomers in the same second are separated by a 30-second settle and a re-read.
  4. Runs cross Modal's 24-hour cap by themselves. A container stops after 23 hours, saves, and spawns its own continuation. Saves are committed at once, so a container that dies loses at most the time since its last save.

Deep CFR's claim, heartbeat and continuation are lifted from the race's launcher rather than imported, because that script builds its own app and Volume at import time.

Grading fans out one container per mark

modal_rung4.py::grade starts one container per kept mark. Each loads the mark's memories, nets and sums and grades three objects with the untouched exact meter:

ObjectWhat it is
exact averagethe true average strategy over every iteration's nets, carried along by an observer from iteration 1. It cannot be rebuilt later without replaying every kept net
Πa fresh policy net fit on the policy memory as it stands at the mark: what a deployed Deep CFR would actually play
current iteratethe strategy this iteration's advantage nets imply, which cycles and is graded to show it

Beside each grade go hands_per_seat (t · K, which lines a mark up with the tabular race), wall_clock_s and the run's config. The local entrypoint writes everything into figures/rung4/grades.json, keyed deep/<t>/<exact|pi|current>. A grade is a pure function of the mark, so regrading is idempotent. Then uv run deep-analysis draws the figures from the grades file. The three graded strategies are also kept on the Volume under frozen/, which is where a release comes from.

Run records

Every Deep CFR arm trained so far, with where it ran, its net and refit settings, and its graded marks, is in docs/notes/rung4-run-log.md. Add a row when an arm is graded. Every number there is read from a file under figures/rung4/, and tests/deepcfr/test_rung4_quotes.py checks the scoreboard against those files. See the run log page for what the arms showed.

Grading a checkpoint by hand
uv run python scripts/minidrawmaha/grade_checkpoint.py runs/mccfr-seed0/solve.npz
uv run python scripts/minidrawmaha/grade_checkpoint.py runs/dcfr/solve.npz --column quadratic

This grades one of a lockstep checkpoint's averages (linear by default, or any extra column the run banked) and prints both seats' best-response values, the exploitability and the expected value, with timings. Budget about 2 GB of memory: the packed table is about 0.3 GB, and the grader holds 1.5 GB of showdown tables.

Releases: frozen strategies the site ships

Each shipped strategy is a GitHub release asset pinned by SHA-256 in one catalogue, with its grade read from a committed file, so every export is reproducible from a clean checkout.

A trained strategy is far too big to commit. The frozen LCFR table alone is an 18 MB file. Yet the site has to say exactly which strategy a page plays and how exploitable it is. Releases are how both are done. The strategy file is published as a GitHub release asset, and the repository commits only its name and SHA-256 digest, plus a pointer to the committed file that holds its grade.

The one place this is all written down is the catalogue, RELEASES in src/drawmaha_solver/minidrawmaha/answer/releases.py. The Releases page is generated from it.

Each entry is a Release with seven fields: name, directory (where /solver's export lands, or None for a strategy shipped another way), tag, asset, sha256, grade_file and grade_key (the object in the grades file holding its exploitability and value_p0).

Note

The ÷ pot arm has since graded lower than anything released (0.068 chips per hand at 10M hands, in figures/rung4/pot_1-256x4-s16000-lr5e-4.json). It is not released yet. The page says which strategy it plays, and the catalogue says what that strategy is.

Why each field exists

  • Published by tag and asset, refused unless the digest matches. fetch_strategy downloads the asset once into ~/.cache/drawmaha-solver (or $XDG_CACHE_HOME/drawmaha-solver) and re-hashes the cached copy on every call. A clean checkout can therefore reproduce every export, and a local copy is identified by its digest (release_of) rather than by its file name.
  • The grade is read, never typed. At export time, read_grade copies the strategy's verdict from its committed grades file into the export's manifest. The tests hold the manifest to the file, so a page quotes a number that has a committed source and never one typed into TypeScript.
  • One provenance block for both exporters. provenance_json writes the strategy block that both /solver's index.json and the deal pack's manifest open with: digest, tag, asset, rule, column, iteration, workers and seed. The two pages can therefore share the words they use about a strategy (describeStrategy in solver-viz).
  • A strategy without a directory cannot reach /solver by accident. minidraw-ranges refuses to export it without an explicit --out.

Cutting a release

  1. Freeze the strategy into a file. For a tabular checkpoint: ``bash uv run python scripts/minidrawmaha/freeze_strategy.py \ runs/modal/lcfr/iter-002000000.npz runs/strategy/strategy-lcfr-2m.npz --grade ` This prints the file's size and SHA-256. --grade re-grades the written file itself (about two minutes, 2 GB), and a faithful freeze matches the checkpoint's own exploitability to the sixth decimal. For Deep CFR, the grader has already saved the three graded objects under frozen/` on the run's Volume (Training runs).
  2. Make sure the grade is committed under figures/. The release will point at it.
  3. Publish the GitHub release with the file as its asset. The tags so far are rung3-lcfr-2m (2026-10-02), rung4-deep-t500 (2026-10-26) and rung4-deep-pot0.5-t200 (2026-10-28).
  4. Add the Release entry with its digest, grade file and key.
  5. Export it to wherever it ships: ``bash uv run minidraw-ranges --release <name> # /solver: web/solver-viz/public/<directory>/ uv run minidraw-deals # /play: the pack for deal_pack.PACK_RELEASE ``
  6. Regenerate the static pages (uv run python scripts/site/make_static_pages.py), so /releases lists it, and commit everything together.
What an export holds
  • /solver's export cuts the strategy by public decision point. Each of the 141 points is one column of the table, and every point of a shape (board cards × discards) stands on one sorted key list written once: 970 keys at b1d0, 10,170 at b2d0 and 100,400 at b2d1. Probabilities are stored as bytes that sum to a scale of 250, so each entry is within 0.4% of the strategy's own number. The whole strategy is 14 MB raw and about 4 MB gzipped, against 57 MB of float32.
  • The deal pack is 600 deals dealt in advance, in six chunks. Each deal carries the strategy's per-mille mix at every node either player can reach, plus all 16 post-draw showdowns. The browser follows the path it is on and reads the vector, so it never has to transcribe the infoset key. The pack holds both holes, so the bot's cards can be read from the network tab. That is accepted for a trainer.

Deploying to Vercel

One build script lays every app, font and static page into public/, framework detection is pinned off, and serve_site.py reproduces the rewrites locally.

The site deploys on Vercel from vercel.json. There is no server and no framework: Vercel runs one build script, serves the folder it writes, and applies a list of rewrites. Every PR gets a preview deployment through Vercel's GitHub integration, which shows up as a status on the PR.

What vercel.json says

{
  "framework": null,
  "buildCommand": "bash scripts/site/vercel_build.sh",
  "outputDirectory": "public",
  "rewrites": [{ "source": "/rules", "destination": "/rules.html" }, "…"]
}

Warning

"framework": null is load-bearing. The repo root has a pyproject.toml, so Vercel would otherwise auto-detect its Python preset. That preset appends a /(.*) → /python catch-all that sends every path to a serverless function that does not exist. The apps, the fonts and the pages would all build fine and then never be served. test_framework_detection_is_pinned_off holds it.

The build command lives in a script because Vercel caps buildCommand at 256 characters.

The build script

scripts/site/vercel_build.sh, in order:

  1. Builds each app. For rung0 rung1 rung2 rung3 solver, it runs npm ci && npm run build in web/<app>-viz and copies dist/ to public/<app>. rung3 builds before solver (test_rung3_builds_before_solver_in_the_build_script).
  2. Copies the hero studies to public/hero.
  3. Copies the fonts. Every app bundles the same @fontsource packages, so the script copies rung 0's woff2 files into the build's fonts folder: Newsreader 400, 500 and 400 italic; Geist 400, 500 and 600; IBM Plex Mono 400 and 600; and Instrument Serif, kept only for /hero.
  4. Copies the shared assets: rung 0's theme.css to /theme.css, plus fonts.css, chip.js and favicon.svg from web/cover/.
  5. Copies the static pages from web/cover/ to the root of public/. Vercel serves index.html at / without a rewrite, and the rewrites map /rules, /benchmarks, /releases, /wiki and /cover to their files.

The static pages are not built here. They are generated ahead of time and committed (The static pages). So a deploy can never produce a page that differs from the one reviewed in the PR.

The two rules that keep negative regret (vanilla, LCFR) beat the two that throw it away (CFR+, DCFR) by 3–4×. Under sampling a row is revisited so rarely that DCFR's halving of negative regret drives it to exactly zero between visits, which is CFR+'s floor — why that happens, and why linear weighting is immune, is the subject of regret rules.

The winner is frozen as the project's tabular answer: LCFR's linear average at 2M iterations, 14.25M probabilities, released as rung3-lcfr-2m and graded at 0.061346 (BR₀ −0.034421, BR₁ +0.157114). In self-play the first seat is worth −0.0906 a hand: the second seat acts last in both rounds and at the draw, and that is worth about a tenth of an ante.

Deep CFR vs the table

Rung 4 replaces the 6.2M ledgers with a network and asks one question at every point of the race's grid:

price = exploitability(Deep CFR's average) ÷ exploitability(LCFR's average), at equal hands per seat

Read it as how many times more exploitable the net is than the table after both have seen the same hands. Below 1 the net is ahead; above 1 the table is. The hands are made equal on purpose — Deep CFR walks K = 10,000 hands per seat per iteration, so its iteration t has seen what LCFR saw at lockstep iteration 1,000·t, and the eight marks t = 10 … 2,000 land exactly on the race's checkpoints. Equal hands is not equal compute: a Deep CFR iteration spends most of its time refitting two nets (see finding 6 of the run log).

The best arm — a 4 × 256 net, a 16,000-step refit at learning rate 5e-4, each regret target divided by its spot's pot — was run to the race's endpoint (figures/rung4/pot_1-256x4-s16000-lr5e-4.json):

The settings every arm shares

All arms are seed 0, K = 10,000 walks per seat per iteration, 50M-row reservoirs and 10,000-row minibatches. So iteration t has seen 10,000·t hands per seat, exactly what LCFR saw at lockstep iteration 1,000·t, and the two can be compared at equal hands.

The rows a run collects never depend on the worker count W: every walk is drawn from (seed, t, seat, walk index). A Mac run and a Modal run of the same config therefore see identical data, and only the refit's floating-point order can differ. That is what lets arms trained on different machines sit in one table.

The eight findings

1. Width was the lever that mattered

At 1M hands, going from 3 × 64 to 3 × 128 cut the exact average from 0.382 to 0.243 (−36%). An extra layer did far less: −2% at 64 wide, −9% at 128 wide. The sweep's rule was fixed before its numbers existed — stay at 3 × 64 unless another size is lower by more than 10% — and it picked 3 × 128. Later arms on the Mac went wider still, to 4 × 256 (0.152).

2. A longer refit helps, by less than a wider net

Doubling the refit from 4,000 to 8,000 Adam steps took 3 × 128 from 0.243 to 0.184 (−24%) and 4 × 256 from 0.152 to 0.134 (−12%). The gain shrinks as the net grows — what you would expect if the small net was under-fit and the large one is near its fit's floor. Early on a longer fit can be worse (4 × 256 at 200k hands: 0.330 against 0.294), so arms are compared at 500k hands or later.

3. The net is far ahead early, then flattens

At 1M hands the best raw arm is 0.133 against the table's 1.131 — an eighth. But from 1M to 5M hands the 16,000-step arm moved 0.133 → 0.115 → 0.108 (−19% over five times the hands) while LCFR fell 1.131 → 0.630 → 0.264 (−77%). That arm was stopped at t = 506; its two averages at t = 500 are the release rung4-deep-t500 that /solver shows. Finding 8 measures where the curves cross.

4. The three graded objects usually order exact < Π < current

The exact average beats Π at every graded mark of every linear arm. The current iterate is usually worst but not always — the 16,000-step arm's current iterate beats both averages at 100k hands. CFR promises convergence of the average only, so none of these orderings is a bug.

5. Per-sample DCFR and CFR+ lose to linear by 17×, and get worse with training

At 500k hands the two screens grade 2.870 and 3.092 against linear's 0.173, and both rise with t. A sampled trainer has no running regret total whose sign the rules could test, so they test each sample's sign instead, and a bad action's positive samples outvote its negative regret. The mechanism, at both rungs, is written up in docs/notes/regret-rules-dcfr-cfrplus.md and on regret rules. Linear is the only rule worth running further.

6. The refit is the iteration

The two refits are about 95% of an iteration's time. Moving them to Apple's GPU (--device mps, PR #61) cut a 4 × 256, 8,000-step refit from 127 s to 34 s per seat. The 16,000-step arms cost about 70 s per seat per refit, about 152 s an iteration.

7. ÷ pot targets: −25% at 2M hands

Three 16,000-step arms differ only in the divisor k:

targets1M2M

What problem it solves. One net fits every spot's regrets with one accuracy budget, and the squared-error loss decides where that budget goes. A regret is in chips, so its size scales with the pot; squared error therefore weighs a spot by how often it is reached × pot². The 52-chip spots count 676 times as much as the 2-chip ante spots reached equally often, and the ante spots — where every hand begins — are fitted last.

Why dividing is free. The divisor is the pot at the decision, shared by every sample at that spot, so it scales the spot's whole regret row by one constant. Regret matching reads only the row's signs and ratios, so it plays the same strategy from the scaled regrets as from the raw ones. What changes is only where the net's errors land.

What k moves. Dividing by pot^k leaves a weight of how often × pot^(2 − 2k). k = ½ weighs a spot by the chips a mistake there costs; k = 1 weighs every spot alike. ÷ √pot is the default for new runs since 2026-10-27.

The caveats. One seed each, and the only noise estimate is two raw runs 0.005 apart; the pot arms' gain is five to six times that. The two weightings are tied at 2M hands. The targets are not otherwise normalised, so dividing also shrinks the gradients the clip of 1.0 sees — part of the gain may be a changed clipping regime rather than the reweighting.

On full Drawmaha: decisions the game forces before training

These come from the audit and are open because no code for the full game exists yet.

  • How the net outputs a discard. A five-card hand has 32 keep/discard subsets. A flat 32-way head, per-card keep/discard logits, or something factorised — and how regret matching behaves over a combinatorial action space, with masking for illegal actions — is an encoding decision with no citation anywhere in the survey (audit item 8).
  • How the non-card state is encoded. Betting history, pot and stack normalisation, position, and both players' public draw counts all have to enter the net's input. Mini-drawmaha's 136-feature encoder is the starting point, not the answer (item 9).
  • How hands are indexed. Suit isomorphism collapses 2,598,960 five-card holdings to 134,459 classes before the deal, but the factor shrinks as board and kept cards fix suits. The standard perfect-indexing algorithm (Waugh 2013) is not yet used (item 7).
  • The exact rule variant. Drawmaha has sub-variants (draw timing, number of betting rounds, odd-chip rules). The project pins one — see full Drawmaha — and every node count and street-boundary claim depends on that choice (item 3).
  • Units and diagnostics. Hold'em calibration anchors (mbb/g, the always-fold baseline) do not transfer to a split-pot bomb pot. The pre-registered protocol reports total chips across both halves, per-half EV and scoop rate, and the terminal-utility table for odd chips and double ties still has to be written (item 16).
  • What is served. Between "trained average-policy net" and "strategy shown or played" sits purification and thresholding — rounding near-zero probabilities before serving — which the project does not do today (item 12).

Scope statements, decided or to be decided

  • Heads-up only. Every guarantee the project relies on — any Nash strategy is safe, the game has one value — is a two-player zero-sum fact. Drawmaha is usually dealt multiway in mixed games, where none of that holds (audit item 18). The project solves heads-up.
  • Equilibrium, not exploitation. The solver plays Nash, which is unbeatable but declines to punish mistakes; Kuhn's exploit tab measures exactly how much that leaves on the table. Opponent modelling and safe exploitation (Restricted Nash Response) are out of scope (item 14).
  • Benchmarking a first-of-its-kind agent. No outside Drawmaha bot exists, so head-to-head baselines must be homegrown: a duplicate-dealt ladder between independently seeded checkpoints plus a trained exploiter, reported as a lower bound on exploitability (item 19). Real-money play against people on poker sites is a terms-of-service violation and not a goal.
  • Rake. Whether the solver targets raked or rake-free play is not stated anywhere; today's games are rake-free.

Three findings from the sweep that shaped the plan

  • Average, never last. Lanes 01, 02 and 04 arrive at the same fact from three directions: in a two-player zero-sum game the time-averaged strategy converges to equilibrium while the current one cycles. Kuhn's exploitability table shows it on the smallest game (Kuhn), and it is why every Deep CFR mark grades the average.
  • The grader is the product. Lane 06's one-sentence summary — self-play produces no labels, so the only principled question is what a perfect adversary wins — is why each game in the project was solved against a known answer before the next was attempted.
  • Nobody had done this. Lane 07 searched papers, code, the commercial solver market and forums for any Drawmaha equilibrium work and found none. The game is documented and dealt online; no solver plays it.

Full Drawmaha, the target game

The game the project exists to solve, what changes from mini-drawmaha, and how a solve will be graded once no exact grader can run.

Everything so far — four solved games and two solves of mini-drawmaha — exists so that a method can meet full Drawmaha already checked. This page states the target, measures how much bigger it is than anything solved, and lays out what the solve needs that mini-drawmaha did not. Nothing here is built yet except the range interface at /solver, which runs on the full game with an illustrative action mix.

The game: a bomb pot with one draw and a pot that pays twice

Standard Drawmaha, heads-up, as the project pins it (README, "The target game"):

  • Each player is dealt 5 hole cards.
  • It is a bomb pot: both players ante and the flop comes out with no preflop betting. The order is ante → flop (3 cards) → betting → one draw (discard 0–5, replace) → turn → betting → river → betting. Three betting rounds, not four.
  • The draw-1 rule. A player who draws exactly one card gets it dealt face up; they may keep it or reject it and take the next card face down.
  • The split pot. Half goes to the best Omaha hand (exactly 2 hole cards + 3 board cards), half to the best five-card poker hand made of the hole cards alone.

The solve's v1 defaults, all configurable: pot-limit with the action set {fold, check/call, pot}; 100 big blinds effective; both players ante, no blinds. The bet-size menu is a config, so a multi-size solve is a re-solve, not a rewrite. The Rules page walks a hand through both readings.

What carries over

  • The algorithm. External-sampling Deep CFR with linear weighting, ÷ √pot regret targets, reservoir memories and a from-scratch refit — the configuration the run log converged on. The rule that came out of rungs 3 and 4 holds: anything sampled uses linear weighting, and DCFR or CFR+ need a reason to be tried again (regret rules).
  • Π as the answer. Mini-drawmaha could keep an exact average in a table; the full game cannot. The policy net Π — fit at each mark on the stored strategy samples — is the only average it can keep, and on mini-drawmaha Π was always more exploitable than the exact average (0.103 against 0.068 at 20M hands). How good Π can be made is a full-game question that mini-drawmaha can still answer cheaply.
  • The rule that a grader never shares code with what it grades. At full scale the grader is an exploiter rather than a walk, but it must still be written against the rules alone.
  • The range interface. /solver already samples 50,000 five-card holdings for a flop, classifies each twice (the five cards alone, and the best two with the board), and draws the range as a grid of inner rows × outer columns. The composition is real to sampling error; the action mix is a smooth function of each hand's two equities and is labelled illustrative. web/solver-viz/src/engine/policy.ts is the one file that changes when the network exists.

Full Drawmaha pipeline

  1. An engine for the full game: deck, streets, the draw with its face-up rule, both scorers, terminal payoffs including odd chips and double ties.
  2. An encoder. Mini-drawmaha's net reads 136 features; the full game needs a representation of five hole cards, the discard, a five-card board, three rounds of betting, and both players' public draw counts. Which cards were kept matters, not only how many — draw-game solvers found discard blockers change strategies.
  3. A discard head. Thirty-two keep/discard subsets as a flat output, or per-card logits, and how regret matching behaves over them, is an open decision (open questions).
  4. Training at scale. The refit is already about 95% of an iteration on mini-drawmaha, so the full game's cost is mostly fitting a bigger net on more rows.
  5. An evaluation that needs no exact grader — below.

How a full-game solve will be graded: pre-registered before training

Training loss is not a progress meter in self-play; the only honest question is what an adversary wins. The README's protocol, fixed before any full-game code exists:

  • A duplicate-dealt head-to-head checkpoint ladder. Each checkpoint plays the next with the cards dealt twice, seats swapped, so card luck cancels and what remains is skill. It shows progress, not distance from equilibrium.
  • A trained RL exploiter against the frozen net, reported as a lower bound on exploitability. A best response found by training is never better than the true one, so whatever it wins, the true exploitability is at least that.
  • Units: total chips across both pot halves, per hand, in big blinds, with per-half EV and scoop rate as split-pot diagnostics.

On mini-drawmaha both ends of this can be checked against the exact grader first — how close a trained exploiter comes to the true best response there is a measurement that can be made today.

Plans

Full Drawmaha has no design document yet. The rung-4 spec, docs/superpowers/specs/2026-10-07-deep-cfr-m1-design.md, lists the full game's engine and encoder as out of scope for its milestone, and its PR table is the live record for current work. The range interface was planned in docs/superpowers/plans/2026-10-07-solver-range-interface.md; the ten design studies it was placed from are under docs/research/range-ui-studies/. Per the docs convention, a change of direction gets a new dated spec — the full-game solve will start with one.
